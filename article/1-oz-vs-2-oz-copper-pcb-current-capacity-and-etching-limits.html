1 oz vs 2 oz Copper PCB: Current Capacity and Etching Limits

Specifying two-ounce versus one-ounce copper foil in printed circuit board (PCB) design creates a critical trade-off between electrical performance and manufacturing precision. While doubling the copper thickness to two ounces significantly boosts current-carrying capacity and improves thermal dissipation, it simultaneously expands etching tolerances, which restricts the use of fine-pitch components and high-density routing. This article explores the exact electrical advantages and manufacturing limitations of choosing between one-ounce and two-ounce copper weights.

Understanding Copper Weight Measurements

In PCB manufacturing, copper thickness is measured in ounces per square foot. This measurement represents the thickness achieved when a specific weight of copper is pressed flat and spread evenly over a one-square-foot area.

  • 1 oz Copper: Equals approximately 1.37 mils (0.00137 inches or 35 micrometers) in thickness. It is the industry standard for most PCB fabrication.
  • 2 oz Copper: Equals approximately 2.74 mils (0.00274 inches or 70 micrometers) in thickness. It is the most common upgrade for power-heavy applications.

Impact on Current-Carrying Capacity

The primary reason engineers specify 2 oz copper over 1 oz copper is to increase the current-carrying capacity of the traces without widening them.

Electrical traces act as tiny resistors. When current flows through a trace, it generates heat. According to IPC-2152 standards, the maximum current a trace can handle is determined by the cross-sectional area of the copper and the maximum allowable temperature rise of the board.

By upgrading from 1 oz to 2 oz copper, you double the cross-sectional area of a trace while keeping its width identical. This yields three distinct advantages:

  1. Higher Current Density: A 2 oz copper trace can safely carry nearly double the electrical current of a 1 oz trace of the same width for a given temperature rise.
  2. Reduced Trace Widths: If board space is limited, using 2 oz copper allows you to shrink the width of high-current power traces by roughly half while maintaining the same current capacity as a wider 1 oz trace.
  3. Lower Resistance and Voltage Drop: Thicker copper reduces electrical resistance. This minimizes voltage drop across long power traces and decreases wasted power in the form of heat, improving the overall thermal management of the PCB.

Impact on Etching Tolerances

While 2 oz copper excels in power delivery, it creates significant challenges during the chemical etching process, negatively impacting trace tolerances and minimum feature sizes.

PCBs are manufactured using a subtractive process where unwanted copper is dissolved away using chemical etchants. Etching does not just happen vertically; it is an isotropic process, meaning the chemicals eat away at the copper sideways as well as downward.

Because 2 oz copper is twice as thick as 1 oz copper, the board must remain in the chemical bath much longer to remove the unwanted material. This prolonged exposure leads to two manufacturing issues:

  • Increased Undercut: As the chemical etches deeper into the 2 oz copper, it also etches sideways underneath the protective photoresist mask. This results in a trapezoidal trace shape where the top of the trace is narrower than the base. The thicker the copper, the more severe this lateral etching becomes.
  • Wider Etch Factor: To compensate for the sideways etching in 2 oz copper, manufacturers require wider minimum trace widths and larger minimum spacing (clearances) between traces. If traces are placed too close together, the etchant cannot properly clear the deep channels between them, leading to electrical shorts. Conversely, if traces are drawn too thin, the prolonged etching will dissolve them completely, causing open circuits.

Typical Design Rule Comparisons

While exact capabilities vary by fabricator, switching from 1 oz to 2 oz copper generally forces the following design rule adjustments:

  • 1 oz Copper: Standard fabrication easily allows for 4 mil (0.1 mm) minimum trace widths and 4 mil minimum spacing. Advanced fabricators can push this down to 3 mil/3 mil.
  • 2 oz Copper: Standard fabrication typically requires a minimum of 6 mil to 8 mil (0.15 mm to 0.2 mm) trace widths and 6 mil to 8 mil minimum spacing.

Choosing the Right Copper Thickness

Deciding between 1 oz and 2 oz copper requires balancing the power needs of your circuit against the physical footprint of your components.

Choose 1 oz copper if:

  • Your design uses fine-pitch components, such as BGA (Ball Grid Array) or small QFN packages, which require traces thinner than 6 mils to route successfully.
  • The board primarily handles low-power digital or analog signals.
  • You need precise impedance control for high-speed data lines, as the steeper trace sidewalls of 1 oz copper provide more predictable impedance calculations.

Choose 2 oz copper if:

  • You are designing power supplies, motor controllers, power distribution boards, or high-wattage LED systems.
  • Your traces need to carry currents exceeding 2 to 3 Amps, and you do not have the physical board space to route extremely wide 1 oz traces.
  • Your design uses larger, standard-pitch components that do not require tight trace-and-space tolerances.
