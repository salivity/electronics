AEC-Q100 Temperature Cycling and Stress Tests for Under-Hood ICs

This article provides a comprehensive breakdown of the rigorous temperature cycling and environmental stress tests mandated by the AEC-Q100 standard for qualifying integrated circuits (ICs) used in under-hood automotive electronics. It covers the essential automotive temperature grades required near the engine block, details the specific parameters for Temperature Cycling (TC), High-Temperature Operating Life (HTOL), and moisture-related stress protocols, and explains how these tests prevent catastrophic failure in harsh automotive environments.

Understanding AEC-Q100 Under-Hood Temperature Grades

Before any stress testing begins, an integrated circuit must be classified into an ambient operating temperature grade. Under-hood electronics—such as Engine Control Units (ECUs), transmission controllers, and turbocharger actuators—are exposed to extreme radiant heat from the engine and freezing ambient conditions. Therefore, they typically require Grade 0 or Grade 1 qualification:

  • Grade 0: -40°C to +150°C ambient operating temperature (Used for components mounted directly on the engine or transmission).
  • Grade 1: -40°C to +125°C ambient operating temperature (Standard for general under-hood electronics).

All subsequent stress tests are scaled according to the target grade of the device.

Preconditioning (PC)

For surface-mount devices (SMDs), AEC-Q100 mandates Preconditioning (Test Method A1) before subjecting the ICs to temperature cycling or moisture tests. This step simulates the stress of the circuit board assembly process. The ICs are baked to remove moisture, soaked in a humid environment based on their Moisture Sensitivity Level (MSL), and then subjected to three cycles of solder reflow temperatures (peaking up to 260°C). This ensures the package does not delaminate or crack during manufacturing before it ever reaches the vehicle.

Temperature Cycling (TC)

Temperature Cycling (Test Method A4) evaluates the mechanical integrity of the IC package and its internal interconnects when exposed to rapid, extreme temperature swings. Because silicon, copper lead frames, gold wire bonds, and epoxy mold compounds expand and contract at different rates (Coefficient of Thermal Expansion mismatch), repeated thermal flexing can cause fatigue and cracking.

  • Test Standard: JESD22-A104
  • Conditions: The air-to-air chamber cycles from -55°C (or -50°C depending on the specific revision) up to the maximum grade temperature (+125°C for Grade 1, +150°C for Grade 0).
  • Duration: 1,000 cycles for Grade 1; 1,000 to 2,000 cycles for Grade 0 (or equivalent thermal shock conditions).
  • Post-Test Requirement: Following TC, the parts undergo Wire Bond Integrity testing to ensure thermal expansion has not lifted or broken the microscopic wires connecting the silicon die to the package leads.

Power Temperature Cycling (PTC)

Power Temperature Cycling (Test Method A5) is required for ICs that dissipate significant power, causing their internal junction temperature to rise rapidly above the ambient temperature. While the environmental chamber cycles the ambient air between -40°C and +125°C (or +150°C), the IC is periodically powered on and off. This creates a worst-case thermal gradient from the inside out, testing the die attach material and silicon integrity over 1,000 cycles.

High-Temperature Operating Life (HTOL)

High-Temperature Operating Life (Test Method B1) is an accelerated life stress test designed to trigger electrical failure mechanisms, such as electromigration, gate oxide breakdown, and threshold voltage shifts, over a simulated 10-to-15-year vehicle lifespan.

  • Test Standard: JESD22-A108
  • Conditions: The ICs are dynamically biased (powered on and actively switching) at their maximum rated operating temperature (+125°C for Grade 1; +150°C for Grade 0) or an even higher junction temperature (\(T_j\)).
  • Duration: Typically 1,000 hours.

High-Temperature Storage Life (HTSL)

Unlike HTOL, High-Temperature Storage Life (Test Method A6) is performed without electrical power applied. The ICs are baked at +150°C to +175°C for 1,000 hours (JESD22-A103). This test checks for thermally activated degradation of the package materials, mold compound stability, and intermetallic growth at the wire bond pads, which can cause brittle fractures over time.

Moisture and Humidity Stress Tests

Under-hood electronics are exposed to rain, road splash, and high humidity, which can penetrate plastic IC packages and corrode the internal metal traces. AEC-Q100 mandates two primary moisture stress tests:

1. Biased HAST (Highly Accelerated Stress Test) or THB (Temperature Humidity Bias)

Test Method A2 evaluates corrosion resistance while the device is electrically biased. Biased HAST (JESD22-A110) is the preferred modern method because it reduces test time.

  • HAST Conditions: +130°C at 85% Relative Humidity (RH) under atmospheric pressure with electrical bias applied for 96 hours (or +110°C for 264 hours).
  • THB Conditions: +85°C at 85% RH with electrical bias for 1,000 hours.

2. Unbiased HAST or Autoclave

Test Method A3 evaluates the moisture resistance of the non-operating package to detect delamination and popcorning effects.

  • Conditions: The unpowered ICs are placed in a pressurized chamber at +130°C and 85% RH for 96 hours (Unbiased HAST, JESD22-A118) or +121°C at 100% RH (2 atmospheres of pressure) for 96 hours (Autoclave).

Electrical Verification Across Temperature

Passing the physical stress tests is not enough. AEC-Q100 requires that before and after these environmental stresses (such as TC, HTOL, and HAST), every single test sample must undergo full electrical parametric testing at room, minimum (-40°C), and maximum (+125°C/+150°C) temperatures. The standard dictates a strict "zero defects" policy: if even a single IC in the sample lot fails to meet its datasheet specifications after temperature cycling or stress testing, the component fails qualification.
