Biodegradable Electronics: Fungal, Cellulose, and Shellac

Electronic waste is a rapidly growing global crisis, largely driven by the disposal of short-lived and single-use electronic devices. To combat this environmental threat, researchers are developing sustainable alternatives to traditional non-recyclable circuit boards. This article explores how scientists are engineering fungal mycelium, cellulose paper, and shellac-based water-soluble substrates to create fully biodegradable electronics that naturally decompose or dissolve after use, drastically reducing global e-waste.

Fungal Mycelium as Circuit Board Substrates

Traditional printed circuit boards (PCBs) are made from fiberglass and epoxy resins that do not degrade in nature. Researchers have discovered that the root-like networks of fungi, known as mycelium, can serve as a viable replacement.

To create these "myceliotronics," scientists grow specific strains of fungi, such as Ganoderma lucidum, on agricultural waste like wood chips or beechwood sawdust. As the fungus consumes the waste, it forms a dense, interwoven mat of mycelium. Researchers harvest the thin outer skin of this mat and dry it out. The resulting material is flexible, naturally flame-retardant, and capable of withstanding the high temperatures required for soldering electronic components, up to 250 degrees Celsius.

Once the mycelial skin is treated and flattened, researchers use physical vapor deposition and laser patterning to apply conductive copper and gold tracks onto the surface. The final product functions just like a standard PCB for low-power devices, such as proximity sensors and battery casings. When the device is no longer needed, the electronic chips can be removed, and the mycelium substrate can be buried in standard garden soil, where it completely decomposes within weeks.

Cellulose Paper in Transient Electronics

Cellulose is the most abundant organic polymer on Earth, making paper a cheap and highly sustainable candidate for disposable electronics. However, standard paper is too porous and rough for microscopic electronic circuits, and it easily absorbs moisture, which can ruin a device.

To overcome this, researchers are extracting cellulose nanofibers (CNF) from wood pulp. By breaking the cellulose down to the nanoscale and reconstituting it, scientists create a transparent, dense, and ultra-smooth film. These nanopaper substrates are treated with biodegradable hydrophobic (water-repelling) coatings to protect them from ambient humidity during their operational lifespan.

Conductive circuits are then printed directly onto the cellulose film using biodegradable inks made from silver nanowires, zinc, or carbon. This method is widely used to manufacture disposable medical diagnostics, smart food packaging, and temporary environmental sensors. At the end of its lifecycle, the cellulose substrate breaks down rapidly through natural microbial action or industrial composting, leaving behind zero toxic residues.

Shellac-Based Water-Soluble Substrates

Another promising material is shellac, a natural bio-resin secreted by the female lac bug on trees in the forests of India and Thailand. Shellac has been used for centuries as a wood finish and was even used in early phonograph records. Today, material scientists are repurposing it for electronics due to its exceptional electrical insulation properties and smooth surface finish.

Researchers dissolve raw shellac in ethanol and cast it into thin, flexible sheets or use it in 3D printers to create custom electronic housings and substrates. Because shellac naturally melts and reforms at relatively low temperatures, conductive traces made of transient metals like magnesium, zinc, or iron can be easily embedded onto its surface without the need for toxic chemical adhesives.

While shellac is naturally water-resistant, its chemical structure allows it to dissolve in alkaline (high pH) water solutions. Researchers are formulating shellac-based composites that remain stable during regular use, even in humid environments, but dissolve completely when submerged in a mild alkaline bath or exposed to specific environmental triggers. This allows for the effortless recovery of valuable microchips, which sink to the bottom of the solution while the substrate washes away harmlessly.

The Impact on Disposable Electronics

The integration of fungal mycelium, cellulose paper, and shellac into electronics manufacturing directly targets the most problematic sector of e-waste: disposable technology. Single-use medical testing kits, RFID shipping tags, point-of-sale displays, and agricultural soil sensors are typically used for only a few days or weeks before being thrown into landfills. By building these devices on organic, water-soluble, and biodegradable substrates, the electronics industry can transition toward a circular economy where temporary gadgets safely return to the earth rather than polluting it for centuries.
