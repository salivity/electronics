Can Carbon Nanotube Transistors Beat Silicon CMOS Efficiency?

This article explores the potential of aligned arrays of semiconducting carbon nanotube field-effect transistors (CNTFETs) to surpass traditional silicon CMOS technology in post-silicon electronics. It provides a quick overview of the energy-delay product (EDP) metric, the physical limitations currently facing silicon transistors, and how carbon nanotubes offer superior electrical properties that could lead to faster and more energy-efficient microprocessors. Finally, it examines the manufacturing hurdles that researchers must overcome to make CNTFETs commercially viable.

Understanding the Energy-Delay Product (EDP)

To determine if a new technology can beat silicon, engineers use the energy-delay product (EDP). This metric measures both the energy consumed by a transistor during a switching event and the time it takes to complete that switch. A lower EDP means a processor can perform calculations faster while using less battery power and generating less heat.

For decades, silicon Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) technology improved its EDP by shrinking in size. However, as silicon transistors approach atomic scales, quantum mechanical effects cause current leakage. This leakage wastes energy and prevents further significant improvements in the silicon EDP.

The Advantage of Aligned Carbon Nanotubes

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are cylindrical molecules made of rolled-up sheets of single-layer carbon atoms. When used as the channel material in a field-effect transistor, semiconducting CNTs offer distinct physical advantages over silicon:

  • Ballistic Transport: Electrons can move through carbon nanotubes with minimal scattering, allowing for much faster switching speeds than silicon.
  • Ultra-Thin Body: With a diameter of roughly one nanometer, CNTs offer superior electrostatic control. This allows the transistor gate to turn the current on and off completely, drastically reducing energy-wasting leakage currents.
  • Low Voltage Operation: Because of their high carrier mobility and excellent gate control, CNTFETs can operate at much lower supply voltages than silicon CMOS, directly lowering energy consumption.

When these nanotubes are arranged in dense, aligned arrays, they can drive enough electrical current to power complex integrated circuits. Theoretical models and early experimental prototypes indicate that aligned-array CNTFETs could achieve an EDP up to ten times better than the ultimate physical limits of silicon CMOS.

Key Challenges to Surpass Silicon

While the physics of carbon nanotubes clearly support a superior energy-delay product, practical manufacturing challenges currently prevent them from replacing silicon on a commercial scale. To surpass silicon in real-world applications, engineers must solve three primary issues:

1. Purity of Semiconducting Nanotubes

When carbon nanotubes are grown, they naturally form as a mix of metallic and semiconducting tubes. Even a tiny fraction of metallic nanotubes in a transistor array will cause short circuits and ruin the energy efficiency. Achieving the required 99.9999% semiconducting purity remains a major chemical processing hurdle.

2. Precise Alignment and Density

To beat the drive current of silicon, CNTs must be packed tightly together in perfectly straight lines. If the tubes are misaligned or spaced too far apart, the transistor will be too slow. Current deposition techniques are improving, but consistently achieving the target density of 100 to 200 nanotubes per micrometer across an entire wafer is difficult.

3. Contact Resistance

Connecting microscopic metal wires to one-nanometer-wide carbon tubes creates high electrical resistance at the contact points. This contact resistance slows down the transistor and wastes energy. Developing new contact materials and geometries is essential to unlocking the intrinsic EDP advantages of CNTFETs.

The Verdict on Post-Silicon Electronics

Aligned arrays of semiconducting carbon nanotube field-effect transistors possess the intrinsic material properties required to surpass the energy-delay products of silicon CMOS. As fabrication methods for sorting, aligning, and contacting nanotubes mature, CNTFETs stand out as one of the most promising candidates to lead the next generation of high-performance, low-power post-silicon electronics.
