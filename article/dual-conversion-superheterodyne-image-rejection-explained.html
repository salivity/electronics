Dual-Conversion Superheterodyne Image Rejection Explained

Dual-conversion superheterodyne receivers solve the fundamental radio engineering trade-off between image rejection and adjacent channel selectivity. This article explains how radio electronics use two distinct local oscillators and intermediate-frequency (IF) filters to eliminate unwanted image frequencies while isolating the desired signal. By stepping down a received radio signal in two separate stages—first to a high intermediate frequency and then to a low intermediate frequency—these receivers achieve exceptional clarity and performance in crowded frequency spectrums.

The Image Frequency Problem

In a standard single-conversion superheterodyne receiver, an incoming radio frequency (RF) signal is mixed with a frequency generated by a local oscillator (LO) to produce a fixed intermediate frequency (IF). Because a mixer produces both the sum and the difference of the inputs, two different RF frequencies can mix with the same LO frequency to create the exact same IF.

One of these is the desired signal, and the other is the unwanted "image frequency," located at a distance of twice the IF away from the desired signal. If the image frequency enters the mixer, it blends directly into the IF stage and causes severe interference that no downstream filter can remove.

The Single-Conversion Compromise

To prevent image interference, the image frequency must be blocked before it reaches the first mixer. However, designing a single-conversion receiver forces a compromise:

  • High IF: Choosing a high IF pushes the image frequency far away from the desired signal. This makes it very easy for the initial RF front-end filter to block the image. However, it is difficult and expensive to build high-frequency IF filters with steep, narrow bandwidths, resulting in poor selectivity against nearby channels.
  • Low IF: Choosing a low IF allows for cheap, extremely sharp IF filters that easily separate closely spaced stations. However, a low IF places the image frequency very close to the desired signal, making it nearly impossible for the front-end RF filter to block the image.

The First Conversion: Rejecting the Image

Dual-conversion receivers eliminate this compromise by dividing the tasks across two stages.

When a signal enters a dual-conversion receiver, it first passes through a basic RF filter. It then enters the first mixer, where it combines with a tunable first local oscillator (LO1). This converts the signal to a high first intermediate frequency (IF1).

Because IF1 is intentionally high, the image frequency is located very far away from the desired signal. The front-end RF filter easily rejects this distant image before it ever hits the mixer. Once the signal is converted to IF1, it passes through a first IF filter (often a crystal or ceramic filter). This filter does not need to be razor-sharp; its primary job is to provide a moderate narrowband bottleneck that blocks out the new, closer image frequency that will be relevant to the second conversion stage.

The Second Conversion: Achieving Selectivity

After passing through the first IF filter, the clean IF1 signal enters a second mixer. Here, it is combined with a fixed-frequency second local oscillator (LO2). This steps the signal down a second time to a much lower intermediate frequency (IF2).

At this low IF2, the receiver can use highly selective, narrowband filters (such as mechanical filters, ceramic filters, or digital signal processing). Because the signal frequency is now low, these IF2 filters can have extremely steep skirts, allowing the receiver to slice out adjacent channel interference sitting just kilohertz away from the target signal.

By splitting the process, the high first IF stage combined with the first LO handles image rejection, while the low second IF stage combined with the second LO handles adjacent channel selectivity.
