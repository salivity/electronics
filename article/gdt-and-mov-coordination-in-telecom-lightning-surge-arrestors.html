GDT and MOV Coordination in Telecom Lightning Surge Arrestors

This article explains how Gas Discharge Tubes (GDTs) and Metal Oxide Varistors (MOVs) work together in multi-stage lightning surge arrestors to protect outdoor telecom electronics. By combining the high current-handling capability of a GDT with the fast clamping speed of an MOV, engineers create a robust defense against destructive transient voltages. This overview covers the roles of each component, the necessity of a decoupling impedance stage, and the step-by-step sequence of how these devices coordinate during a lightning strike to ensure uninterrupted telecommunication operations.

The Role of Each Component

To understand why GDTs and MOVs are paired, it is necessary to look at their individual strengths and weaknesses:

  • Gas Discharge Tube (GDT): A GDT acts as a voltage-dependent switch. It can handle massive surge currents (often tens of kiloamperes) and has near-zero leakage current and very low capacitance during normal operation, which prevents signal distortion on high-frequency telecom lines. However, GDTs are relatively slow to turn on (taking microseconds), allowing a sharp spike of voltage to pass through before the gas ionizes.
  • Metal Oxide Varistor (MOV): An MOV acts as a voltage-dependent resistor that clamps voltage spikes much faster than a GDT (taking nanoseconds). While it reacts quickly, an MOV cannot handle as much surge current as a GDT without degrading over time. It also suffers from higher parasitic capacitance and continuous leakage current when connected directly to power or signal lines.

The Multi-Stage Topology

In an outdoor telecom surge arrestor, the GDT serves as the primary protection stage (closest to the outside line/surge source), and the MOV serves as the secondary protection stage (closest to the sensitive telecom equipment).

If you connect a GDT and an MOV directly in parallel, the system will fail to coordinate. Because the MOV reacts faster and typically has a lower clamping threshold than the GDT’s initial spark-over voltage, the MOV will clamp the transient voltage first. By clamping the voltage, the MOV prevents the voltage from rising high enough to trigger the GDT. As a result, the MOV absorbs the entire lightning energy alone and is rapidly destroyed.

Achieving Coordination via Decoupling Impedance

To force the GDT and MOV to share the workload properly, a decoupling element—typically a series inductor (for AC/DC power lines) or a series resistor/PTC thermistor (for signal lines)—is placed between the primary GDT and the secondary MOV.

Step-by-Step Surge Coordination Sequence

When a lightning-induced surge travels down an outdoor telecom line toward the equipment, the coordinated multi-stage arrestor operates in four distinct phases:

  1. Initial Surge Arrival (Nanoseconds): The transient voltage hits the arrestor. The GDT is too slow to react immediately, so the initial wave passes through the decoupling element and reaches the MOV.
  2. Secondary Clamping: The MOV instantly recognizes the overvoltage and begins to conduct, clamping the voltage to a safe level for the downstream telecom electronics.
  3. Voltage Build-up and Hand-off: As the surge current increases through the MOV, it must also flow through the series decoupling inductor or resistor. According to Ohm's Law (\(V = I \times R\)) and the laws of inductance (\(V = L \times \frac{dI}{dt}\)), this high current creates a significant voltage drop across the decoupling element. Consequently, the voltage at the input side (where the GDT is located) rises rapidly while the voltage at the output side (where the MOV is located) remains clamped.
  4. Primary Crowbar Action (Microseconds): The rising voltage ahead of the decoupling element quickly reaches the GDT's spark-over threshold. The gas inside the GDT ionizes, turning it into a virtual short circuit (an arc). The voltage across the GDT drops to its very low arc voltage (typically 10V to 30V), diverting the vast majority of the massive lightning current safely to the ground and relieving the stressed MOV.

Preventing Follow-Current (Series Configurations)

While parallel coordination with a decoupling element is standard for signal and low-voltage lines, GDTs and MOVs are also frequently wired directly in series on AC power feeds or high-voltage DC telecom buses.

In a series configuration, the GDT blocks the normal system voltage from reaching the MOV, eliminating the MOV’s continuous leakage current and preventing thermal runaway. When a surge occurs, both devices trigger together. Once the surge passes, the MOV quickly returns to a high-resistance state, cutting off the power supply’s "follow-current" so the GDT can successfully extinguish its internal arc and reset for future events.
