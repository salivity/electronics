How 4-Quadrant SMUs Test Photovoltaic Cells and Diodes

This article provides a comprehensive breakdown of how four-quadrant source measure units (SMUs) operate to characterize photovoltaic cells and diodes. It explains the fundamentals of the four-quadrant operational matrix, details the internal electronic mechanisms that allow an SMU to simultaneously source voltage while sinking current, and outlines how these capabilities are directly applied to extract critical performance parameters from semiconductor devices.

Understanding the Four-Quadrant Matrix

Standard power supplies typically operate in only one quadrant: they output positive voltage and positive current to power a device. A four-quadrant SMU, however, acts as both a precision power supply and a dynamic electronic load, mixed with a high-resolution digital multimeter.

The four quadrants are defined by the polarity of the voltage and current at the device terminals:

  • Quadrant I (+V, +I): Positive voltage and positive current. The SMU delivers power to the device (sourcing).
  • Quadrant II (-V, +I): Negative voltage and positive current. The SMU absorbs power from the device (sinking).
  • Quadrant III (-V, -I): Negative voltage and negative current. The SMU delivers power to the device in reverse polarity (sourcing).
  • Quadrant IV (+V, -I): Positive voltage and negative current. The SMU absorbs power from the device (sinking).

The Mechanics of Sourcing Voltage and Sinking Current

To simultaneously source voltage and sink current, the SMU operates in Quadrant II or Quadrant IV. Internally, a four-quadrant SMU utilizes a bidirectional, bipolar output stage, typically built around a linear Class-AB amplifier combined with precision feedback loops.

When an SMU is programmed to source a specific positive voltage across an active device—such as an illuminated solar cell—the device itself generates a current that wants to flow out of its positive terminal and into the SMU's positive terminal.

Here is how the SMU handles this simultaneously:

  1. Voltage Clamping: The SMU's internal operational amplifier uses a voltage-control feedback loop to lock the output terminals at the exact programmed voltage.
  2. Current Absorption (Sinking): Because the external device is pushing current into the SMU, the voltage at the terminal naturally tries to rise above the programmed setpoint. The SMU’s feedback loop immediately detects this micro-increase. To maintain the exact voltage setpoint, the output stage transistors adjust their bias to draw the incoming current inside the SMU, routing it safely to the internal power supply return (ground) or dissipating it as heat across internal power transistors.
  3. Simultaneous Measurement: As this current is drawn into the SMU to keep the voltage stable, it passes through precision internal shunt resistors or feedback ammeters. This allows the SMU to measure the exact amount of current it is sinking while holding the sourced voltage steady.

Characterizing Photovoltaic (PV) Cells

Photovoltaic cells are active devices when exposed to light, meaning they generate power rather than consume it. To characterize a solar cell, engineers must sweep the voltage across the cell and measure the resulting current to generate an I-V (Current-Voltage) curve.

During this test, the SMU operates primarily in Quadrant IV (+V, -I):

  • Short-Circuit Current (\(I_{sc}\)): The SMU sources 0 V (acting as a short circuit) and sinks the maximum current generated by the illuminated cell.
  • Maximum Power Point (\(P_{max}\)): The SMU sweeps the sourced positive voltage upward. As it does, it continuously sinks the current coming from the cell and measures it. The point where the product of sourced voltage and sunk current is highest represents the cell's maximum efficiency.
  • Open-Circuit Voltage (\(V_{oc}\)): The SMU continues increasing the sourced positive voltage until the voltage matches the cell's internal potential. At this exact point, current stops flowing (0 A), and the SMU measures the open-circuit voltage.

Without the ability to sink current while forcing a positive voltage, the SMU would not be able to hold the solar cell at the specific voltage biases required to map its power generation capabilities.

Characterizing Diodes

Standard diodes, Zener diodes, and LEDs are passive devices, meaning they generally consume power. However, a complete characterization requires sweeping the device from deep reverse bias to forward conduction, utilizing Quadrants I and III.

  • Forward Bias (Quadrant I: +V, +I): The SMU sources a positive voltage and sources positive current into the diode's anode. The SMU measures the current to determine the forward voltage drop (\(V_f\)) and series resistance.
  • Reverse Bias and Breakdown (Quadrant III: -V, -I): To test reverse leakage current and breakdown voltage, the SMU sources a negative voltage. This forces a small negative current to flow through the diode. Both voltage and current are negative, meaning the SMU is actively delivering power in reverse polarity.
  • Photodiodes (Quadrant IV or II): If the diode being tested is a photodiode exposed to light, it acts like a miniature solar cell. Engineers will apply a reverse bias or a small forward bias while the photodiode generates photocurrent. The SMU must then sink this generated current while maintaining the bias voltage to measure the photodiode's responsivity and quantum efficiency.
