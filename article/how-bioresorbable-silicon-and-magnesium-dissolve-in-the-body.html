How Bioresorbable Silicon and Magnesium Dissolve in the Body

Transient medical electronics are revolutionizing post-surgical care by monitoring vital internal conditions and then harmlessly vanishing inside the patient. This article explores the chemical mechanisms that allow bioresorbable silicon nanomembranes and magnesium traces to dissolve in human bodily fluids after their monitoring duties are complete. It covers the process of hydrolysis, the role of protective encapsulation layers, and how the human body safely metabolizes and excretes the resulting microscopic byproducts without the need for extraction surgery.

The Role of Transient Medical Electronics

After a major operation, doctors often need to monitor internal temperature, pressure, and electrical activity at the surgical site. Traditional medical implants require a second surgery to remove the device once the patient heals, increasing the risk of infection and tissue trauma. Transient electronics solve this problem by using materials engineered to function for a specific timeframe before breaking down completely inside the body. The core functional components of these devices are typically silicon nanomembranes, which act as the semiconductors, and magnesium traces, which serve as the conductive wiring and antennas.

The Dissolution of Silicon Nanomembranes

Traditionally, silicon is thought of as a permanent, non-degradable material because bulk silicon takes centuries to break down. However, when silicon is shaved down into ultra-thin sheets known as nanomembranes—often just a few nanometers thick—it becomes bioresorbable.

Inside the human body, silicon nanomembranes dissolve through a chemical reaction called hydrolysis. Surrounding biofluids, which are mostly water and electrolytes, react directly with the silicon. The water molecules break the chemical bonds of the silicon, converting the solid nanomembrane into orthosilicic acid (\(Si(OH)_4\)) and microscopic quantities of hydrogen gas.

Orthosilicic acid is a naturally occurring, non-toxic compound frequently found in the human diet through water and various foods. At a normal physiological pH of 7.4 and a body temperature of 37 degrees Celsius, these nanomembranes dissolve at a predictable rate of a few nanometers per day.

The Breakdown of Magnesium Traces

Magnesium is the ideal metal for the wiring and electrodes of transient implants because it is an essential nutrient the human body already uses for bone structure, muscle function, and enzyme regulation.

Like silicon, magnesium degrades via hydrolysis when exposed to bodily fluids. The water in the body oxidizes the magnesium traces, transforming the solid metal into magnesium hydroxide (\(Mg(OH)_2\)) and a small, harmless amount of hydrogen gas. However, the human body is rich in chloride ions. These chloride ions rapidly react with the magnesium hydroxide, converting it into highly soluble magnesium chloride (\(MgCl_2\)). Because magnesium dissolves much faster than silicon—often within hours or days—it requires careful structural management to ensure the device works for the necessary post-surgical window.

Controlling the Lifespan of the Implant

If a device needs to monitor a patient for two weeks, the silicon and magnesium cannot begin dissolving the moment they are implanted. Engineers control the dissolution timeline using protective encapsulation layers.

The entire electronic device is coated in biodegradable polymers, such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), silk fibroin, or thin layers of silicon dioxide. These encapsulating materials act as a timed barrier. Biofluids must first eat through this outer shell before they can reach the magnesium traces and silicon nanomembranes inside. By adjusting the thickness and chemical composition of the outer coating, scientists can program the exact day the internal electronics will be exposed to bodily fluids and begin to dissolve.

Safe Absorption and Excretion

Once the protective barrier is breached and hydrolysis converts the silicon and magnesium into water-soluble compounds, the body's natural waste management systems take over.

The minuscule amount of hydrogen gas produced during the chemical breakdown diffuses safely through the surrounding tissues and is absorbed into the bloodstream, eventually being exhaled by the lungs. The orthosilicic acid from the silicon and the magnesium ions from the conductive traces are absorbed into the blood and processed by the kidneys. Because the total mass of a transient implant is exceptionally small—often weighing less than a milligram—the resulting chemical byproducts are well below daily recommended dietary intakes and are easily excreted through urine without causing toxicity or strain on the organs.
