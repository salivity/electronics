How CMOS AND Logic Gates Work in Digital Electronics

In digital electronics, a Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) AND logic gate evaluates logical conjunctions—producing a high output only when all inputs are high—by physically combining a CMOS NAND gate with a CMOS NOT gate (inverter). Because standard CMOS transistor physics naturally inverts signal levels, engineers cannot build a single-stage AND gate efficiently. Instead, this article explains how a two-stage architecture uses four transistors to execute a NAND operation and two additional transistors to invert that result, yielding a true AND output with minimal power consumption and high noise immunity.

Why CMOS Requires a Two-Stage Design

A CMOS circuit relies on two types of transistors: PMOS (p-type) and NMOS (n-type). By nature, these transistors operate best as inverting switches:

  • PMOS transistors pass strong high signals (Logic 1) and are activated by a low gate voltage (Logic 0). They form the pull-up network connected to the voltage source (VDD).
  • NMOS transistors pass strong low signals (Logic 0) and are activated by a high gate voltage (Logic 1). They form the pull-down network connected to the ground (GND).

If you attempt to build a single-stage AND gate by putting NMOS transistors in the pull-up network and PMOS in the pull-down network, the output voltage degrades significantly. Therefore, to maintain full voltage swings and crisp digital signals, CMOS logic must use inverting stages. To achieve a non-inverting AND function, two inverting stages are connected in series: a NAND stage followed immediately by an inverter stage.

Stage 1: The CMOS NAND Gate

The first stage takes the initial inputs (Input A and Input B) and evaluates them using four transistors: two PMOS and two NMOS.

  1. The Pull-Up Network (PMOS in Parallel): Two PMOS transistors are connected in parallel between VDD and the stage's output node. If either Input A or Input B is low (Logic 0), at least one PMOS transistor turns on. This creates a direct path from VDD to the intermediate output, pulling the signal high (Logic 1).
  2. The Pull-Down Network (NMOS in Series): Two NMOS transistors are connected in series between the intermediate output node and GND. For the output to be pulled low (Logic 0), both Input A and Input B must be high (Logic 1) simultaneously. This turns on both NMOS transistors, creating a continuous path to the ground while both PMOS transistors turn off.

At the end of Stage 1, the circuit has evaluated the conjunction of inputs A and B, but the result is inverted. If both inputs are 1, the intermediate output is 0. In all other cases, the intermediate output is 1.

Stage 2: The CMOS Inverter (NOT Gate)

To turn the NAND result into a true AND output, the intermediate signal is fed directly into the gates of a standard CMOS inverter, which consists of two transistors: one PMOS and one NMOS.

  1. When Stage 1 Output is Low (0): This occurs only when both original inputs (A and B) are high (1). The low intermediate signal turns on the inverter's PMOS transistor and turns off its NMOS transistor. This connects the final output to VDD, resulting in a final high output (Logic 1).
  2. When Stage 1 Output is High (1): This occurs when one or both original inputs are low (0). The high intermediate signal turns off the inverter's PMOS transistor and turns on its NMOS transistor. This connects the final output to GND, resulting in a final low output (Logic 0).

Complete Conjunction Evaluation

By cascading these two stages using a total of six transistors, the CMOS AND gate accurately evaluates logical conjunctions:

  • Inputs 0 and 0: NAND stage outputs 1 -> Inverter outputs 0.
  • Inputs 0 and 1: NAND stage outputs 1 -> Inverter outputs 0.
  • Inputs 1 and 0: NAND stage outputs 1 -> Inverter outputs 0.
  • Inputs 1 and 1: NAND stage outputs 0 -> Inverter outputs 1.

This two-stage combination ensures that at any steady state, there is never a direct path between the power supply and the ground, allowing the CMOS AND gate to evaluate logic with near-zero static power dissipation.
