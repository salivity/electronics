How Differential Amplifiers Reject Common-Mode Noise

Differential amplifiers are fundamental electronic circuits designed to amplify the voltage difference between two input signals while simultaneously suppressing any voltage that is identical on both inputs. This article provides a comprehensive overview of the internal mechanics of a differential amplifier, focusing on the symmetrical transistor configuration known as the long-tailed pair, the critical role of the constant current source, and the mathematical principles behind the Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR). By understanding these core concepts, you will see exactly how these circuits isolate weak, desired signals from noisy environments.

The Core Principle: Differential vs. Common-Mode Signals

To understand how a differential amplifier works, you must first distinguish between two types of signals:

  1. Differential-Mode Signals: These are two separate signals applied to the two inputs (non-inverting and inverting) that are equal in magnitude but opposite in phase. The amplifier is designed to amplify the difference between these two voltages.
  2. Common-Mode Signals: These are signals that appear simultaneously and in-phase on both inputs with the exact same amplitude. In practical electronics, common-mode signals are almost always unwanted external noise, such as 60 Hz power line hum or electromagnetic interference picked up equally by two adjacent wires.

The mathematical formula for the output of an ideal differential amplifier is:

V_out = A_d (V_in+ - V_in-)

Where A_d is the differential gain, V_in+ is the non-inverting input, and V_in- is the inverting input. If the same noise voltage is added to both inputs, the subtraction cancels the noise out entirely, leaving only the difference to be multiplied by the gain.

Circuit Symmetry: The Long-Tailed Pair

At the heart of a differential amplifier is a circuit topology called a "long-tailed pair." This configuration uses two matched transistors (either Bipolar Junction Transistors or MOSFETs) whose emitters (or sources) are connected together.

For the amplifier to reject noise effectively, the two halves of the circuit must be identical. The collector resistors must have the exact same resistance, and the two transistors must have identical electrical characteristics. In integrated circuits, this is achieved by fabricating the components simultaneously on the same piece of silicon.

How the Constant Current Source Blocks Noise

The connected emitters of the two transistors are tied to ground (or a negative voltage rail) through a constant current source, which forms the "tail" of the long-tailed pair. This constant current source is the secret to common-mode rejection.

Here is how the circuit reacts to different signals:

When a Differential Signal is Applied: If the voltage at Input A goes up and the voltage at Input B goes down by the same amount, Transistor A conducts more current, and Transistor B conducts less. Because the increase in one matches the decrease in the other, the total current flowing into the shared tail remains constant. The voltage at the collectors changes dramatically in opposite directions, resulting in high amplification of the difference.

When Common-Mode Noise is Applied: If a noise spike causes the voltage at both Input A and Input B to rise simultaneously, both transistors will attempt to conduct more current at the same time. However, they share the tail connected to the constant current source. Because the current source refuses to allow more total current to flow, and because the circuit is symmetrical, neither transistor can increase its current.

Instead, the voltage at the shared emitter node simply rises to match the common-mode input voltage. Because the current through the transistors does not change, the voltage drop across the collector resistors does not change. Consequently, the common-mode noise does not appear at the output.

Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

In the real world, no two transistors or resistors are 100% identical, and no current source has infinite impedance. Because of these tiny manufacturing imperfections, a minuscule amount of common-mode noise will leak through to the output.

Engineers quantify a differential amplifier's ability to block noise using the Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR). It is the ratio of the differential gain (A_d) to the common-mode gain (A_cm), usually expressed in decibels (dB):

CMRR = 20 log10 (A_d / A_cm)

A standard operational amplifier might have a CMRR of 80 dB to 100 dB. A CMRR of 100 dB means the desired differential signal is amplified 100,000 times more than the unwanted common-mode noise.

Practical Applications

Because of their ability to extract tiny signals from high-noise environments, differential amplifiers are used extensively in modern electronics:

  • Professional Audio (XLR Cables): Microphones send audio over three-pin XLR cables. One wire carries the normal audio signal, and a second wire carries an inverted version of the same signal. Both wires pick up the same stage noise. At the mixing console, a differential amplifier subtracts the two inputs, canceling the noise and doubling the audio signal.
  • Medical Instrumentation: Electrocardiograms (ECGs) measure microvolt-level electrical signals from the heart. The human body acts like an antenna for 60 Hz AC power noise. Differential amplifiers reject the massive 60 Hz common-mode voltage on the body, allowing doctors to see the tiny differential voltage of the heartbeat.
  • High-Speed Data: Technologies like USB, Ethernet, and HDMI use differential signaling (twisted pairs of wires) to transmit data at gigabit speeds without corruption from internal computer noise.
