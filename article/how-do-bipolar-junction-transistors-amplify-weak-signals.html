How Do Bipolar Junction Transistors Amplify Weak Signals?

This article explains the fundamental process of how bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) amplify weak analog signals in electronic circuits. You will learn about the three-terminal structure of a BJT, the essential concept of DC biasing, the physics of current gain, and how a small fluctuating input current is converted into a much larger output voltage. By understanding the relationship between the base, collector, and emitter, you will see how these components serve as the backbone of analog amplification.

The Three-Terminal Structure

A bipolar junction transistor consists of three doped semiconductor regions known as the Emitter, Base, and Collector. They come in two main configurations: NPN and PNP, with NPN being the most common for basic amplification.

To understand amplification, you must look at the function of each pin:

  • Emitter: Heavily doped to inject charge carriers (electrons in an NPN transistor) into the base.
  • Base: A very thin and lightly doped middle layer that controls the flow of carriers from the emitter to the collector.
  • Collector: Moderately doped to collect the carriers coming through the base and send them to the output circuit.

Setting the Stage: DC Biasing

A BJT cannot amplify a weak analog signal on its own; it requires a constant Direct Current (DC) power source to function. The transistor does not magically create energy. Instead, it uses a weak input signal to control a large reservoir of power provided by the DC supply.

Before feeding an analog signal into the transistor, engineers apply a specific DC voltage to the base. This is called biasing.

An NPN transistor has a built-in voltage barrier between the base and the emitter, typically around 0.7 volts for silicon. If the weak analog signal is only a few millivolts, it is not strong enough to overcome this 0.7V barrier. Biasing adds a steady 0.7V DC voltage to the base, keeping the transistor partially "on" in what is known as the active region. The weak Alternating Current (AC) analog signal is then superimposed, or "rides," on top of this steady DC voltage.

The Physics of Current Gain

In its active region, the BJT operates as a current-controlled current regulator. The base-emitter junction is forward-biased (allowing current to flow easily), while the collector-base junction is reverse-biased.

When the weak analog signal fluctuates, it causes minute changes in the base-emitter voltage. This pushes electrons from the emitter into the base. Because the base region is extremely thin and lightly doped, less than 1% to 5% of these electrons recombine with the base material to form the base current.

The remaining 95% to 99% of the electrons are swept across the thin base by the strong electric field of the collector, forming a much larger collector current.

The ratio of the collector current to the base current is the transistor's current gain, known as Beta (\(\beta\)) or \(h_{FE}\). A typical BJT might have a Beta of 100. This means that a tiny fluctuation of 1 microampere of current from your weak analog signal at the base will cause a 100-microampere fluctuation in the collector current.

Converting Current to Voltage Output

For most electronic applications, such as audio systems or radio receivers, the goal is to amplify voltage, not just current. To achieve this, a resistor (known as the load resistor) is placed in series with the collector and the DC power supply.

As the amplified collector current flows through this load resistor, it creates a voltage drop across the resistor according to Ohm's Law (\(Voltage = Current \times Resistance\)).

Here is the step-by-step cycle of voltage amplification in a standard Common-Emitter configuration:

  1. The weak analog input signal rises slightly in voltage.
  2. This causes a small increase in the base current.
  3. Due to the transistor's Beta, this creates a massive increase in the collector current.
  4. The large collector current flows through the collector resistor, causing a large voltage drop across that resistor.
  5. The output voltage (measured at the collector) drops significantly in direct proportion to the tiny rise at the input.

As the input signal waves up and down, the output voltage waves down and up at a much larger scale. Capacitors are then used at the input and output of the circuit to block the steady DC biasing voltages, leaving only the newly amplified, pure AC analog signal to move on to the next stage of the electronic device.
