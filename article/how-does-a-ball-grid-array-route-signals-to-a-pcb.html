How Does a Ball Grid Array Route Signals to a PCB?

This article explains how Ball Grid Array (BGA) packages successfully route hundreds of electrical signals from a microscopic silicon die to a printed circuit board (PCB). We will cover the internal architecture of the BGA, how the package substrate redistributes microscopic connections, the role of the underside solder spheres, and the multi-layer PCB breakout strategies—such as dog-bone fanout and via-in-pad—that complete the signal path in modern electronics.

The Core Challenge: Pitch Translation

A silicon die contains hundreds or thousands of input/output (I/O) pads spaced extremely close together. A standard PCB cannot be manufactured with copper traces narrow enough to connect directly to these microscopic pads. The BGA package solves this problem by acting as a spatial translator, spreading these dense connections out over a wider, manageable grid on the bottom of the chip.

Step 1: Die-to-Substrate Connection

Signal routing begins by moving the electrical paths from the silicon die to the BGA’s internal package substrate (often called an interposer). This is achieved using one of two primary methods:

  • Wire Bonding: The silicon die is mounted upright on the substrate. Microscopic gold or copper wires are arcs-welded from the outer edges of the die down to bonding pads on the substrate.
  • Flip-Chip: The silicon die is flipped upside down. Tiny conductive bumps are placed directly on the die's surface, aligning with matching pads on the substrate. This method eliminates long bond wires, reducing inductance and allowing signals to exit from the entire surface area of the die, not just the perimeter.

Step 2: Redistribution Within the BGA Substrate

Once the signals enter the BGA substrate, they must be routed to the underside of the package. The substrate itself is a miniature, high-density, multi-layer circuit board.

Inside this substrate, ultra-thin copper traces and microscopic vertical interconnects (microvias) fan the signals outward. Tightly packed connections directly beneath the small silicon die are routed horizontally across different layers and vertically down to the bottom surface of the package. This process transforms a tiny, hyper-dense cluster of connections into a wider, uniformly spaced grid of metal pads.

Step 3: The Underside Solder Spheres

Attached to the bottom grid of the BGA substrate are tiny spheres of solder, known as solder balls. Unlike traditional chip packages that only use the four outer edges for metal pins, a BGA utilizes the entire bottom surface area of the package. This area-array approach allows a BGA to hold hundreds of connections in a much smaller footprint.

During PCB assembly, the BGA is placed onto matching copper pads on the motherboard. The board passes through a reflow oven, melting the solder spheres. Surface tension causes the molten solder to self-align the chip perfectly with the PCB pads. When it cools, the solder solidifies, forming both the mechanical bond and the direct electrical pathway from the BGA substrate to the PCB.

Step 4: PCB Fanout and Signal Escape

Once the signals pass through the solder balls onto the top layer of the PCB, they face a final routing hurdle. The inner rows of solder balls are completely surrounded by outer rows, making it impossible to route all signals away using only the top layer of the circuit board.

Engineers use a multi-layer PCB and specific "escape routing" or "fanout" techniques to free the signals from the grid:

  • Outer Row Routing: The outermost ring of solder pads is routed directly away from the chip using standard copper traces on the top layer of the PCB.
  • Dog-Bone Fanout: For BGAs with moderate spacing, a short trace is drawn from the solder pad to a nearby plated-through hole (via). The pad-trace-via combination looks like a dog bone. The via drops the signal down to an internal layer of the PCB, where it can travel outward underneath the surrounding solder balls without causing a short circuit.
  • Via-in-Pad: For high-density BGAs where solder balls are too close together for dog-bone routing, the via is drilled directly into the center of the PCB copper pad. The via is filled with conductive or non-conductive epoxy and plated over with copper so it is completely flat. The solder ball sits directly on top of it, sending the signal straight down into the inner PCB layers immediately upon contact.
  • Microvias and HDI: In complex devices like smartphones, High-Density Interconnect (HDI) boards are used. Lasers drill microscopic vias that only span one or two layers deep (blind and buried vias). By stacking or staggering these microvias across multiple internal PCB layers, hundreds of signals can methodically escape the dense BGA footprint and route to the rest of the electronic system.
