How Ground Loops Cause 60 Hz Hum in Audio and Electronics

Ground loops and the resulting 60-hertz hum occur when interconnected audio or instrumentation devices share multiple paths to ground, creating an unintended closed electrical loop. When a slight voltage difference—known as a potential difference—exists between the chassis grounds of two separate pieces of equipment, an alternating current flows through the shielding of the interconnecting signal cables. Because these systems rely on the ground path as a zero-volt reference point, this unwanted current directly modulates the signal path, manifesting as a persistent 60-hertz audible hum in audio gear or corrupted baseline readings in sensitive measurement electronics.

The Anatomy of a Ground Loop

In an ideal electrical system, "ground" is a universal, zero-volt reference point. Safety regulations require that the metal chassis of mains-powered equipment be connected directly to the earth ground via the third prong of an AC power cord.

When you connect two grounded devices—such as an audio mixer and a powered amplifier, or a sensor array and an oscilloscope—using a standard unbalanced cable, you link their chassis grounds together through the cable's shield. At this moment, a closed conductive loop is formed:

  1. From the first device's chassis, through its power cord, to the wall outlet's earth ground.
  2. Through the building’s electrical ground wiring to the second device's wall outlet.
  3. Up the second device's power cord to its chassis.
  4. Back to the first device's chassis via the shield of the interconnecting signal cable.

This circular path is a ground loop. By itself, a loop of wire is harmless, provided no current flows through it. However, potential differences between the ground points guarantee that current will flow.

How Potential Differences Arise

The earth ground wiring inside a building does not have zero electrical resistance. Like any physical conductor, copper wiring has a small but measurable impedance.

When multiple devices are plugged into different outlets—especially outlets on different circuit breakers—the physical distance between their ground connections increases. Stray currents from other appliances on the building's power grid, internal leakage currents from power supplies, and electromagnetic induction from nearby AC power lines all dump small amounts of alternating current into the building's ground wiring.

Because of Ohm’s Law (\(V = I \times R\)), as this stray 60-hertz AC current (\(I\)) flows through the natural resistance (\(R\)) of the building's ground wires, it creates a voltage drop (\(V\)). Consequently, the chassis of Device A might sit at a ground potential of 0.05 volts, while the chassis of Device B sits at 0.02 volts. A potential difference of 0.03 volts now exists between the two pieces of equipment.

Signal Corruption and the 60 Hz Hum

Electricity always seeks the path of least resistance to equalize potential differences. Because the shield of an interconnecting audio or instrumentation cable has a very low resistance, the 60-hertz current driven by the potential difference flows directly through the cable shield from one chassis to the other.

In unbalanced connections (like standard RCA or 1/4-inch TS cables), the cable shield serves two purposes: it protects the inner signal wire from outside interference, and it acts as the return path (the 0V reference) for the audio or data signal itself.

As the unwanted 60-hertz current travels along the cable shield, it encounters the internal resistance of the shield wire. This creates a small 60-hertz voltage drop across the length of the cable. The receiving device cannot distinguish between the intended signal coming down the center wire and the fluctuating voltage on the reference shield. It simply measures the difference between the two. Because the reference ground is now oscillating at 60 times per second (the frequency of standard North American AC power), that 60-hertz oscillation is added directly to the desired signal.

In audio systems, this is amplified and heard as a low-frequency 60-hertz hum—often accompanied by a 120-hertz buzz or higher harmonics caused by rectifiers in the equipment's power supplies. In instrumentation electronics, this manifests as a sine-wave ripple superimposed over precise DC measurements, ruining data integrity.

Magnetic Induction in the Loop

Even if the building's ground points are at the exact same voltage potential, a ground loop can still generate a 60-hertz hum through electromagnetic induction.

The closed loop formed by the power cords and the interconnect cable acts as a single-turn secondary winding of a transformer. Every AC power cord, wall wart, and power transformer in the room emits a 60-hertz magnetic field. As these magnetic fields expand and collapse through the physical area of the ground loop, Faraday’s Law of Induction dictates that an alternating current is induced inside the loop. Just like the current caused by conductive potential differences, this induced current flows through the signal cable's shield, creating the exact same voltage drop and resulting hum.

Eliminating the Potential Difference

To stop the 60-hertz hum without compromising safety (never remove the third grounding prong on an AC plug), the conductive loop must be broken or the signal path must be separated from the chassis ground.

  • Star Grounding: Plugging all interconnected equipment into the exact same power strip minimizes the physical distance and wire resistance between their earth grounds, drastically reducing the potential difference.
  • Balanced Interconnects: Using balanced cables (such as XLR or TRS) separates the chassis shield from the signal return path. The signal is carried on two inner wires (positive and inverted), and the receiving device uses differential amplification to cancel out any 60-hertz noise that was equally induced onto both wires.
  • Isolation Transformers (DI Boxes): Placing a 1:1 isolation transformer in the signal path physically breaks the direct metallic connection between the two devices. The signal passes through magnetically, while the ground loop is completely severed.
