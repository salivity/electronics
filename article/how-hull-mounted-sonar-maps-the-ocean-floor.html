How Hull-Mounted Sonar Maps the Ocean Floor

This article explains how hull-mounted marine sonar electronics use stave-mounted piezoelectric hydrophones and time-delay beamformers to create detailed maps of the ocean floor. By transmitting sound waves toward the seabed and capturing the returning echoes across organized arrays of sensors, modern sonar systems can accurately calculate ocean depth and underwater topography. The following sections break down the mechanics of hydrophone staves, the physics of piezoelectricity, the mathematics of time-delay beamforming, and how these components work together to turn sound into visual seafloor data.

The Role of Hull-Mounted Sonar

Hull-mounted sonar systems are permanently fixed to the bottom of a marine vessel. To map the ocean floor, the system uses active sonar. A projector transmits an acoustic pulse, or "ping," downward into the water. This sound wave travels until it hits the seabed and bounces back toward the ship as an echo. The receiving electronics must then capture this echo and determine both how long it took to return and the exact angle from which it arrived.

Staves and Piezoelectric Hydrophones

To capture the returning echoes effectively, the sonar receiver is divided into "staves." A stave is a linear row of individual underwater microphones called hydrophones, arranged side-by-side along the hull. Grouping hydrophones into staves allows the system to capture acoustic data across a wide physical area.

Each hydrophone within a stave relies on piezoelectric materials—typically specialized ceramics—to function. Piezoelectricity is the ability of certain materials to generate an electric charge in response to applied mechanical stress.

  1. Acoustic Impact: The returning sound wave from the ocean floor travels through the water as a pressure wave.
  2. Mechanical Stress: This pressure wave strikes the hydrophone, slightly compressing the piezoelectric ceramic inside.
  3. Electrical Conversion: The compression generates a tiny, proportional voltage.

The marine sonar electronics amplify this voltage and convert it from an analog electrical signal into digital data. Because a stave contains multiple hydrophones, the system records slightly different versions of the same echo at each sensor location.

How Time-Delay Beamformers Work

If an echo returns from directly beneath the ship, the sound wave hits all the hydrophones in a stave at the exact same time. However, if the echo bounces back from an angle (such as a submarine canyon wall off to the side of the ship), the sound wave will reach the hydrophones on one end of the stave fractions of a millisecond before it reaches the hydrophones on the other end.

This is where the time-delay beamformer comes in. Beamforming is a signal processing technique used to control the directionality of the reception of a signal. A time-delay beamformer allows the sonar system to "listen" in specific directions without physically moving the hydrophone staves.

The beamformer processes the digital signals from the stave using the following steps:

  • Applying Delays: To listen for an echo coming from a specific angle, the beamformer applies precise, artificial time delays to the signals of the hydrophones that the sound wave hit first.
  • Signal Alignment: These programmed delays match the natural propagation delay of the sound wave traveling through the water. As a result, the signals from all hydrophones in the stave for that specific angle are now aligned in time.
  • Summation: The aligned signals are added together (summed). Signals coming from the target angle reinforce each other, creating a strong, clear reading. Signals and noise coming from other directions remain out of alignment and cancel each other out.

By rapidly applying different sets of time delays to the same recorded data, the electronics simultaneously create multiple "beams" pointing at dozens or hundreds of different angles beneath the ship.

Generating the Ocean Floor Map

To construct a 3D map of the ocean floor, the sonar electronics combine the data from the hydrophone staves and the time-delay beamformer for every ping.

The beamformer determines the precise angle of the returning echo. Meanwhile, the system's internal clock measures the two-way travel time—the exact duration from when the ping left the ship to when the aligned echo peaked in the beamformer.

Knowing the speed of sound in water, the sonar computer uses the travel time to calculate the distance to the seabed along each specific beam angle. This creates a "swath" of depth points across the ocean floor perpendicular to the ship's path. As the vessel moves forward, the hull-mounted sonar repeats this process, stitching consecutive swaths together to produce a continuous, high-resolution topographic map of the seabed.
