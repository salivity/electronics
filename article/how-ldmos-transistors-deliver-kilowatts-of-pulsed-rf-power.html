How LDMOS Transistors Deliver Kilowatts of Pulsed RF Power

Laterally Diffused Metal Oxide Semiconductor (LDMOS) transistors are the backbone of high-power radio frequency (RF) amplification in cellular base-stations and broadcast electronics. This article explains the specific physical architecture, thermal management techniques, and electrical characteristics that allow silicon-based LDMOS devices to reliably generate kilowatts of pulsed RF power without suffering from electrical breakdown or thermal destruction.

The Extended Drift Region and High Voltage Handling

Generating kilowatts of RF power requires operating at high drain supply voltages, typically between 28V and 65V. Standard silicon transistors break down at these voltages, so LDMOS transistors use a specialized physical layout to survive.

During manufacturing, dopants are diffused laterally into the silicon to create an exceptionally short channel length alongside an extended, lightly doped "drift region" between the gate and the drain. The short channel allows electrons to move rapidly, supporting high-frequency RF operation. Meanwhile, the extended drift region spreads out the intense electric field generated by the high drain voltage. This prevents the device from experiencing avalanche breakdown, enabling it to handle massive voltage swings necessary for kilowatt-level output.

Eliminating Inductance with Grounded Source Vias

At RF frequencies, microscopic amounts of stray inductance on the source terminal can severely degrade a transistor's gain and efficiency. Traditional transistors use tiny wire bonds to connect the source to the ground, which introduces unwanted inductance.

LDMOS architecture solves this by connecting the source terminal directly to the bottom of the silicon die. Manufacturers achieve this by etching deep trenches (vias) through the silicon substrate and filling them with conductive metal, or by using a heavily doped sinker diffusion. Because the source sits flush against the grounded metal baseplate of the package, parasitic source inductance is virtually eliminated. This allows the transistor to maintain high power gain even at UHF and microwave frequencies.

Managing Capacitance with Faraday Shields

For an RF transistor to deliver kilowatts of power efficiently, the input signal at the gate must be isolated from the massive power swings at the drain. If the capacitance between the gate and drain (feedback capacitance) is too high, the amplifier becomes unstable and inefficient.

LDMOS transistors incorporate a metal layer called a Faraday shield (or field plate) positioned between the gate and the drain drift region. This shield is electrically connected to the grounded source. It acts as an electrostatic barrier that intercepts the electric field lines between the drain and gate, drastically reducing feedback capacitance. Additionally, the field plate helps reshape the electric field in the drift region, further increasing the voltage the transistor can withstand.

Surviving Extreme VSWR and Load Mismatches

In broadcast towers and cellular base-stations, environmental factors like ice on an antenna or a damaged coaxial cable can cause the transmitted kilowatt signal to reflect right back into the transistor. This is known as a high Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR).

LDMOS is uniquely suited to survive these reflections. Unlike bipolar transistors, which conduct more current as they get hotter and quickly destroy themselves in a process called thermal runaway, LDMOS has a negative temperature coefficient at high current levels. As an LDMOS die heats up, the mobility of electrons in the silicon decreases, which naturally limits the current. Modern LDMOS devices can absorb reflected energy at a 65:1 VSWR mismatch—meaning nearly all the kilowatt power is bounced back into the transistor—without failing.

Pulsed Operation and Thermal Dissipation

Delivering kilowatts of continuous power generates enough heat to melt silicon. LDMOS achieves extreme power levels primarily in pulsed applications, or by handling high Peak-to-Average Power Ratio (PAPR) signals common in 4G, 5G, and digital television broadcasts.

In pulsed operation, the transistor fires a multi-kilowatt burst of RF energy for a fraction of a millisecond and then rests. During the "off" period, the heat generated in the microscopic active region of the silicon must be evacuated instantly. Because the LDMOS source is bonded directly to the bottom of the wafer (thanks to the grounded source design), there are no insulating layers blocking heat flow. Furthermore, the silicon die is mechanically thinned to mere micrometers during manufacturing and bonded to advanced copper-tungsten or copper-molybdenum flanges, allowing rapid thermal transfer out of the transistor and into the external heatsink.

Internal Impedance Matching

At kilowatt power levels, the natural input and output impedance of a large silicon transistor drops to fractions of an ohm. Connecting a 0.5-ohm transistor directly to a standard 50-ohm circuit board results in massive power loss.

To solve this, high-power LDMOS packages include internal pre-matching networks. Inside the transistor housing, manufacturers place precise arrays of high-grade MOS capacitors and bond wires of exact lengths, which act as inductors. These components transform the minuscule impedance of the silicon die up to a manageable level (typically between 2 and 10 ohms) before the signal ever leaves the transistor package, allowing engineers to easily extract the full kilowatt potential on the external circuit board.
