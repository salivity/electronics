How LLC Resonant Converters Achieve Zero-Voltage Switching

LLC resonant converters achieve zero-voltage switching (ZVS) by utilizing a specialized resonant tank circuit composed of two inductors and a capacitor to shape the current and voltage waveforms. By operating in the inductive region of this resonant tank, the current lags behind the applied voltage, creating a brief window during the dead time between switch transitions. During this dead time, the lagging current discharges the parasitic output capacitance of the switching MOSFETs before they are turned on. Because the voltage across the switch drops to zero prior to conduction, the overlap of high voltage and high current is eliminated, drastically minimizing switching losses in high-efficiency power electronics.

The Role of the LLC Resonant Tank

At the heart of the LLC converter is the resonant tank, which consists of three main reactive components:

  • Resonant Inductor (\(L_r\)): Works in series with the capacitor to create the primary resonance.
  • Resonant Capacitor (\(C_r\)): Blocks DC current and shapes the sinusoidal current waveform.
  • Magnetizing Inductor (\(L_m\)): Built into the transformer, it acts as a shunt inductor and is crucial for achieving ZVS across a wide load range.

In traditional Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) converters, switches are forced on and off while carrying high currents and voltages, known as "hard switching." This overlap causes significant energy dissipation as heat. The LLC tank solves this by converting the square-wave voltage from the primary switches into a smooth, sinusoidal current waveform.

Operating in the Inductive Region

For ZVS to occur, the LLC converter must operate in the inductive region of its impedance curve. The LLC tank has two resonant frequencies: a higher frequency determined by \(L_r\) and \(C_r\), and a lower frequency that includes \(L_m\).

To maintain an inductive state, the switching frequency is kept above the lowest resonant frequency boundary. When the resonant tank is inductive, the resonant current lags behind the voltage applied by the switches. This phase lag is the fundamental requirement for zero-voltage switching. It ensures that when a switch turns off, the current is still flowing in a direction that will naturally prepare the opposing switch to turn on without voltage stress.

The Step-by-Step ZVS Mechanism

In a typical half-bridge LLC configuration using two primary MOSFETs (let's call them \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\)), ZVS is achieved through a precise sequence of events during the switching transition:

  1. Turn-Off Transition: Assume \(Q_1\) is currently conducting and \(Q_2\) is off. The controller turns \(Q_1\) off.
  2. The Dead Time Interval: Both \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) are kept off for a brief, precisely calculated period known as "dead time." This prevents shoot-through currents.
  3. Discharging Parasitic Capacitance: Every MOSFET has an inherent parasitic output capacitance (\(C_{oss}\)). Because the tank is operating in the inductive region, the magnetizing current (\(I_m\)) continues to flow even after \(Q_1\) turns off. This current is forced to flow through the parasitic capacitances of the MOSFETs. It charges the \(C_{oss}\) of \(Q_1\) up to the input voltage and simultaneously discharges the \(C_{oss}\) of \(Q_2\) down to zero volts.
  4. Body Diode Conduction: Once the capacitor across \(Q_2\) is fully discharged, the remaining inductive current forces the internal body diode of \(Q_2\) to conduct. This clamps the voltage across \(Q_2\) at practically zero volts.
  5. Zero-Voltage Turn-On: While the body diode is conducting and holding the drain-to-source voltage at zero, the gate signal is applied to turn \(Q_2\) on. Because there is no voltage across the switch when the gate turns on, the switching loss (\(P = V \times I\)) is virtually zero.

Bonus: Secondary Side Zero-Current Switching (ZCS)

In addition to primary-side ZVS, LLC converters can simultaneously achieve Zero-Current Switching (ZCS) on the secondary side rectifiers. When the converter operates at or below the primary resonant frequency, the secondary rectifier current naturally resonates back to zero before the switches change state. This eliminates the reverse recovery losses typically associated with output diodes, further boosting overall system efficiency.

Impact on Power Electronics

By eliminating turn-on switching losses via ZVS and reducing rectifier losses via ZCS, LLC resonant converters routinely achieve efficiencies exceeding 95%. Furthermore, because switching losses are no longer a major thermal bottleneck, engineers can design these converters to operate at much higher switching frequencies. Higher frequencies allow for the use of significantly smaller transformers and capacitors, resulting in the high power density required for modern EV chargers, server power supplies, and renewable energy inverters.
