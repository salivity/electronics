How MEMS Gyroscopes Use the Coriolis Effect to Measure Rotation

Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) gyroscopes are essential components in modern stabilization electronics, enabling everything from smartphone screen rotation to drone flight control and automotive safety systems. This article explores the internal mechanics of MEMS gyroscopes, specifically focusing on how microscopic vibrating proof masses utilize the Coriolis effect to accurately detect and measure angular velocity. By translating physical rotational forces into readable electrical signals, these tiny sensors provide the critical data needed to keep complex electronic systems balanced and oriented.

The Role of the Vibrating Proof Mass

At the core of a MEMS gyroscope is a microscopic mechanical element known as a proof mass. Unlike traditional mechanical gyroscopes that rely on a heavy, continuously spinning wheel to maintain orientation, a MEMS gyroscope uses a silicon proof mass suspended by microscopic springs.

To function, the gyroscope must first establish a constant state of motion. Electrostatic forces generated by "drive" electrodes push and pull the proof mass, causing it to oscillate back and forth along a single plane—known as the drive axis. This continuous, rapid vibration is maintained at a specific resonant frequency, ensuring the mass is always moving at a known velocity.

Applying the Coriolis Effect

The Coriolis effect is a physical phenomenon that occurs when an object is in motion within a rotating frame of reference. When the object moves along one axis and the entire system rotates, an apparent force pushes the object perpendicular to both the direction of its motion and the axis of rotation.

Inside a MEMS gyroscope, the vibrating proof mass serves as the moving object. As long as the gyroscope remains completely still, the proof mass simply vibrates back and forth along its drive axis. However, when an external force causes the gyroscope to rotate, the Coriolis effect acts upon the vibrating mass. This force pushes the proof mass sideways, shifting its movement onto a secondary, perpendicular plane known as the sense axis. The faster the rate of rotation (angular velocity), the stronger the Coriolis force, and the further the proof mass is pushed along the sense axis.

Converting Motion into Electrical Signals

To make this physical movement useful for stabilization electronics, the gyroscope must convert the displacement of the proof mass into an electrical signal. This is achieved through capacitive sensing.

Surrounding the sense axis of the proof mass are fixed "sense" electrodes, which form microscopic parallel-plate capacitors with the mass itself. When the Coriolis force pushes the proof mass along the sense axis, the distance between the mass and the fixed electrodes changes. Moving closer to an electrode increases the capacitance, while moving further away decreases it.

Processing Data for Stabilization Electronics

The changes in capacitance inside the MEMS structure are extremely small, often measured in attofarads. An Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) housed within the gyroscope package detects these minute capacitance differentials and converts them into an analog or digital voltage signal.

Because the Coriolis force is directly proportional to the angular velocity, the strength of this resulting voltage signal corresponds precisely to how fast the device is rotating. The ASIC filters out background noise, compensates for temperature variations, and sends the clean angular velocity data to the device's main processor. In stabilization electronics—such as camera gimbals, flight controllers, or vehicle electronic stability control—the processor uses this real-time data to immediately trigger corrective actions, counteracting unwanted movement and maintaining stability.
