How Miniature Circuit Breakers Interrupt Fault Currents

Miniature circuit breakers (MCBs) protect electrical distribution systems by using three core internal mechanisms to detect and stop dangerous electrical currents. First, a thermal mechanism uses a bimetallic strip to stop slow, sustained overloads. Second, a magnetic mechanism uses a solenoid to instantly interrupt massive short-circuit currents. Finally, an arc-extinguishing chamber safely dissipates the dangerous electrical arc created when the internal contacts separate. Together, these components ensure distribution electronics remain safe from fire and equipment damage.

The Thermal Tripping Mechanism (Overload Protection)

The thermal mechanism is designed to handle prolonged overcurrents, which occur when a circuit draws slightly more electricity than it is rated for over a sustained period.

Inside the MCB, the electrical current flows through a bimetallic strip made of two different metals bonded together, typically brass and steel. Because these two metals expand at different rates when heated, the strip bends when excessive current causes it to warm up.

If the overload persists, the bimetallic strip bends far enough to physically push a trip bar. This unlatches the operating mechanism, pulls the electrical contacts apart, and breaks the circuit. Because heating takes time, this mechanism prevents the breaker from tripping during harmless, temporary power surges, such as when a motor first starts up.

The Magnetic Tripping Mechanism (Short-Circuit Protection)

Short circuits cause a sudden, massive spike in electrical current that can destroy distribution electronics in milliseconds. The thermal mechanism is too slow to stop this, so the MCB relies on an electromagnetic mechanism for instantaneous interruption.

Current flows through a wire coil (solenoid) wrapped around a movable iron plunger. Under normal electrical loads, the magnetic field generated by the coil is too weak to move the plunger.

However, when a short circuit occurs, the massive surge of fault current creates a powerful magnetic field instantly. This field forces the iron plunger forward to strike the trip lever, immediately separating the contacts and cutting off the power in a fraction of a second.

The Spring-Loaded Operating Mechanism

Both the thermal and magnetic mechanisms rely on a central, spring-loaded latch system to physically separate the contacts. When you flip an MCB switch to the "ON" position, you are compressing an internal spring that is held in place by a mechanical latch.

When either the bimetallic strip bends or the magnetic plunger strikes the trip bar, this latch is released. The stored energy in the spring snaps the moving contact away from the fixed contact at high speed. Even if the external toggle switch is physically held in the "ON" position from the outside, this "trip-free" internal mechanism will still separate the contacts to guarantee safety.

The Arc Extinguishing Mechanism

Separating two contacts carrying a heavy fault current does not immediately stop the flow of electricity. As the contacts pull apart, the electricity ionizes the air between them, creating a superheated bridge of plasma known as an electric arc. If left unchecked, this arc allows the fault current to continue flowing and will melt the breaker.

To truly interrupt the fault current, the MCB must extinguish this arc. As the contacts separate, the arc is forced upward by magnetic fields and thermal expansion along metal rails called arc runners.

The runners guide the arc into an "arc chute" (or splitter chamber), which consists of a series of parallel, insulated metal plates. When the single large arc hits these plates, it is sliced into a series of smaller arcs. Splitting the arc increases its electrical resistance, stretches it out, and rapidly cools it down. Once the voltage of the power supply is no longer strong enough to sustain these cooled, divided arcs, the plasma dissipates, and the fault current is completely interrupted.
