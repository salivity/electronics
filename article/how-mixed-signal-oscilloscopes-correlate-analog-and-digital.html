How Mixed-Signal Oscilloscopes Correlate Analog and Digital

This article provides a comprehensive overview of how mixed-signal oscilloscopes (MSOs) correlate continuous analog waveforms with sixteen channels of parallel digital bus timing in embedded electronics. It explores the architecture behind synchronized timebases, the mechanics of analog-to-digital and comparator-based acquisitions, cross-domain triggering techniques, and the hardware deskewing processes required to align both domains accurately for efficient system-level debugging.

The Synchronized Acquisition Architecture

At the core of a mixed-signal oscilloscope is a shared, unified acquisition system. Unlike connecting a standalone logic analyzer to a traditional oscilloscope, an MSO drives both its analog channels and its sixteen digital channels using a single master sample clock.

When a trigger event occurs, the MSO captures data across all active channels simultaneously and stores it in time-aligned acquisition memory. Because both domains rely on the exact same internal clock reference, the oscilloscope plots the high-resolution analog waveforms and the 16-bit digital logic states on a single, unified horizontal display grid. This eliminates the need to manually match timestamps between two different instruments.

Processing Analog Integrity vs. Digital Logic

To correlate these two domains, the MSO must process incoming signals differently while keeping them locked in time:

  • Analog Channels: Signals entering the analog channels pass through attenuators and preamplifiers before reaching an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). The ADC typically uses 8-bit to 12-bit vertical resolution to capture the exact voltage levels, overshoot, ringing, ground bounce, and rise times of the waveform.
  • 16 Digital Channels: The sixteen digital channels are routed through a specialized logic probe pod. Instead of using multi-bit ADCs, each digital channel uses a high-speed 1-bit comparator. The user defines a specific logic threshold voltage (such as 3.3V CMOS or 1.8V TTL). The comparator simply registers a logic "1" if the signal is above the threshold or a logic "0" if it is below.

By sacrificing vertical voltage resolution on the sixteen digital channels, the MSO can dedicate memory and processing power to tracking the fast timing transitions of a wide parallel bus alongside a few high-resolution analog signals.

Deskewing for Precise Time Correlation

Electricity travels at different speeds through different types of cables and internal circuitry. An analog passive probe and a 16-channel digital logic pod have different propagation delays. If left uncorrected, a digital transition might appear on the screen several nanoseconds before or after the analog event that caused it.

MSOs solve this through channel deskewing. Internally, the oscilloscope applies factory-calibrated delay offsets to align the digital comparator paths with the analog ADC paths. For high-speed embedded systems, engineers also perform manual deskewing by connecting both an analog probe and the digital channels to a single fast-edge reference signal, adjusting the time offset in the oscilloscope settings until the edges align perfectly on the display.

Cross-Domain Triggering

The most powerful aspect of an MSO in embedded electronics is cross-domain triggering, which allows events in the digital domain to trigger captures in the analog domain, and vice versa.

Engineers can configure the sixteen digital channels as a parallel bus and set a pattern trigger for a specific 16-bit hexadecimal address or data value. When the embedded processor writes that specific value to the parallel bus, the MSO triggers. Because of the shared timebase, the engineer can immediately look at the analog channels at that exact nanosecond to see if the simultaneous switching of the sixteen digital lines caused a voltage droop on the power rail or induced crosstalk on a nearby analog sensor line.

Conversely, you can set an analog trigger to catch a "runt" pulse or a voltage glitch on a clock line, and instantly observe the sixteen digital channels to see how that analog anomaly corrupted the parallel data bus transaction.

Bus Decoding and Signal Integrity Analysis

Once the data is captured and aligned, the MSO translates the raw ones and zeros of the sixteen digital channels into a human-readable bus display. It groups the sixteen individual logic traces into a single virtual bus trace, displaying decoded hexadecimal, binary, or ASCII values directly beneath the analog waveforms.

When a parallel bus fails to read data correctly in an embedded system, the MSO allows the engineer to pinpoint the root cause across domains. If the decoded bus shows an invalid state, the engineer can place an analog probe directly onto the suspect digital line. By viewing the same signal as both a logic state and an analog waveform simultaneously, it becomes clear whether the timing error was caused by a software bug or an analog signal integrity issue like impedance mismatch, slow slew rate, or electromagnetic interference.
