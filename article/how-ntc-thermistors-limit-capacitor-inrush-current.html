How NTC Thermistors Limit Capacitor Inrush Current

Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) thermistors protect electronic power supplies by suppressing the massive surge of electrical current that occurs when AC power is first applied. This article explains why input filter capacitors cause dangerous inrush currents, how the temperature-dependent resistance of an NTC thermistor safely restricts this initial flow, and how the component transitions into a low-resistance state during normal circuit operation.

The Cause of Capacitor Inrush Current

When an electronic device is plugged in and turned on, the AC power goes through a rectifier to charge large input filter capacitors. When these capacitors are completely discharged, they act like a short circuit the instant power is applied. Because their initial opposition to current (impedance) is nearly zero, they draw an enormous spike of electricity known as inrush current. Without protection, this sudden surge can exceed the rated limits of the circuit, blowing input fuses, damaging rectifier diodes, and pitting switch contacts.

Cold State: Restricting the Initial Surge

To prevent this damage, an NTC thermistor is placed in series with the AC input line before the capacitors. "Negative Temperature Coefficient" means the device's electrical resistance decreases as its temperature increases.

When the electronics are off, the NTC thermistor is at ambient room temperature. In this "cold" state, the thermistor possesses a high electrical resistance. The moment AC power is applied, this high resistance sits directly in the path of the incoming electricity. According to Ohm's law, this added resistance restricts the flow of current, forcing the discharged capacitors to fill up at a controlled, safe rate rather than all at once.

Warm State: Allowing Normal Operation

Once the capacitors are charged—a process that takes only fractions of a second—the high resistance is no longer needed and would waste energy if it remained. The NTC thermistor solves this automatically through self-heating.

As the initial current flows through the thermistor, it generates heat. This rapid rise in temperature causes the thermistor’s resistance to drop dramatically. Within moments of the power being applied, the thermistor reaches its "warm" operating state, where its resistance falls to near zero (typically a fraction of an ohm). In this low-resistance state, the thermistor essentially becomes invisible to the circuit, allowing normal operating current to pass through with minimal power loss or voltage drop.

The Cool-Down Limitation

While NTC thermistors are effective and inexpensive, they rely entirely on thermal physics. When the electronic device is turned off, the thermistor requires time to cool down and return to its high-resistance state. This cool-down period usually takes between 30 seconds and two minutes. If AC power is turned off and immediately turned back on while the thermistor is still hot, its resistance will still be low, and it will fail to limit the new inrush current surge. In high-wattage systems, engineers often place a time-delay relay in parallel with the thermistor to bypass it once the capacitors are charged, allowing the thermistor to cool down while the device is still running.
