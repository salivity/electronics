How Nyquist Stability Criterion Predicts Circuit Oscillations

The Nyquist stability criterion is a graphical technique used in control systems and electronic engineering to determine the stability of a closed-loop feedback system. This article provides a comprehensive overview of how this mathematical principle predicts unwanted oscillations in electronic circuits. By examining the open-loop frequency response on a complex plane, engineers can identify whether a feedback loop will remain stable or degrade into self-sustaining oscillations. We will explore the mechanics of loop gain, the importance of the critical (-1, 0) point, the role of Cauchy's argument principle, and how phase and gain margins are utilized to design reliable, oscillation-free electronics.

The Fundamentals of Closed-Loop Feedback

To understand how the Nyquist criterion predicts oscillations, one must first understand the basic structure of a closed-loop feedback system. An electronic amplifier with feedback takes a portion of its output signal and combines it with the input signal. The behavior of this system is governed by its closed-loop transfer function:

T(s) = A(s) / [1 + A(s)β(s)]

In this equation, A(s) represents the open-loop gain of the amplifier, and β(s) represents the feedback factor. The product A(s)β(s) is known as the "loop gain." The denominator of this equation, 1 + A(s)β(s), is the characteristic equation of the system. The roots of this characteristic equation are the closed-loop poles, which dictate the stability of the circuit.

The Condition for Oscillation

In any feedback system, instability and oscillations occur if the closed-loop transfer function has poles in the Right-Half Plane (RHP) of the complex s-plane. Physically, RHP poles correspond to signals that grow exponentially over time rather than decaying, leading to oscillation or saturation.

Looking at the characteristic equation, if the loop gain A(s)β(s) equals -1 at any specific frequency, the denominator (1 + A(s)β(s)) becomes zero. Division by zero causes the closed-loop gain to approach infinity. In practical electronics, an infinite gain means the circuit can generate an output signal with zero input signal. This is the exact condition for self-sustaining oscillation.

In terms of magnitude and phase, the value -1 on the complex plane corresponds to a magnitude of 1 (or 0 dB) and a phase shift of -180 degrees. If the open-loop system amplifies a signal by at least 1 while simultaneously shifting its phase by -180 degrees, negative feedback turns into positive feedback. The circuit will reinforce its own noise at that specific frequency, resulting in continuous oscillation.

Mapping the Nyquist Plot

Finding the closed-loop poles directly from the characteristic equation can be mathematically difficult for complex circuits. The Nyquist stability criterion bypasses this by using the open-loop frequency response—which is easily measured or simulated—to infer the behavior of the closed-loop system.

A Nyquist plot is generated by sweeping the frequency of the input signal from negative infinity to positive infinity and plotting the resulting complex values of the open-loop gain A(jω)β(jω) on a polar coordinate system. The real part of the transfer function is plotted on the horizontal x-axis, and the imaginary part is plotted on the vertical y-axis. Each point on the resulting contour represents the magnitude and phase of the open-loop response at a specific frequency.

The Critical (-1, 0) Point and Cauchy's Principle

Because oscillation occurs when A(s)β(s) = -1, the coordinates (-1, 0) on the complex plane become the critical point of interest on a Nyquist plot.

The Nyquist criterion is based on Cauchy's argument principle from complex analysis. In simple terms, it relates the number of unstable closed-loop poles to the number of times the Nyquist plot encircles the critical (-1, 0) point. The mathematical formula for the criterion is:

Z = N + P

  • Z is the number of unstable closed-loop poles (in the RHP). For the circuit to be stable and free of oscillations, Z must equal zero.
  • P is the number of unstable open-loop poles already present in the system before feedback is applied.
  • N is the number of clockwise encirclements the Nyquist plot makes around the (-1, 0) point. (Counter-clockwise encirclements are counted as negative values).

Predicting Oscillations

Engineers use the Z = N + P formula to predict oscillations in two primary scenarios:

  1. Open-Loop Stable Systems (P = 0): Most electronic amplifiers are stable when operated without feedback, meaning P = 0. For the closed-loop system to remain stable (Z = 0), N must also be zero. Therefore, the Nyquist criterion states that if the Nyquist plot of an open-loop stable system intersects or encircles the (-1, 0) point in a clockwise direction, the closed-loop circuit will oscillate. If the plot does not encircle (-1, 0), the circuit is stable.
  2. Open-Loop Unstable Systems (P > 0): Some complex systems are inherently unstable in an open-loop configuration. Here, P is greater than zero. To achieve stability (Z = 0), the value of N must be negative (N = -P). This means the Nyquist plot must encircle the (-1, 0) point in a counter-clockwise direction an exact number of times equal to the number of unstable open-loop poles. If it fails to do so, the system will oscillate or latch up.

Gain and Phase Margin on the Nyquist Plot

In real-world electronics, component values drift due to temperature, aging, and manufacturing tolerances. Simply avoiding the (-1, 0) point is not enough; the Nyquist plot must stay a safe distance away from it. If the plot passes too close to (-1, 0), the circuit will exhibit "ringing"—damped oscillations that appear in response to sudden input changes.

The Nyquist plot visually represents two vital safety metrics:

  • Gain Margin: This measures how much the open-loop gain can be increased before the plot hits the (-1, 0) point. It is found by looking at the point where the Nyquist plot crosses the negative real axis (where the phase is -180 degrees). If it crosses at -0.5, the gain can be doubled before reaching -1, providing a 6 dB gain margin.
  • Phase Margin: This measures how much additional phase shift the circuit can tolerate before oscillating. It is determined by finding the point where the Nyquist plot intersects the "unit circle" (where the magnitude of the gain is equal to 1). The angle between this intersection point and the negative real axis is the phase margin.

By using the Nyquist stability criterion to ensure the open-loop response does not improperly encircle the (-1, 0) point, and by maintaining adequate gain and phase margins, engineers can reliably predict and prevent oscillations in closed-loop electronic systems.
