How Phase-Change Pads and Liquid Metal Reduce Heat

This article explores how silicone-free phase-change thermal interface pads and liquid-metal compounds minimize thermal contact resistance in modern electronics. When processors generate heat, microscopic surface imperfections between the processor die and the heatsink trap air, which acts as a thermal insulator. This overview covers the mechanics of surface roughness, how phase-change materials transition from solid to liquid to fill these gaps without siloxane contamination, and how liquid-metal alloys leverage extreme thermal conductivity and surface wetting to provide the lowest possible thermal resistance.

Understanding Thermal Contact Resistance

Even when polished to a mirror finish, the surfaces of a silicon processor die and a metal heatsink are not completely flat. Under a microscope, these surfaces consist of jagged peaks and valleys. When pressed together, physical contact only occurs at the highest peaks, leaving up to 90% of the interface separated by microscopic air gaps.

Because air has a very low thermal conductivity (approximately 0.024 W/m·K), these pockets of trapped air create a bottleneck known as thermal contact resistance. To transfer heat efficiently, a thermal interface material (TIM) must displace every bit of this trapped air and minimize the distance between the die and the heatsink, known as the bond-line thickness (BLT).

Silicone-Free Phase-Change Thermal Pads

Phase-change materials (PCMs) are engineered to be solid at room temperature for easy application, but transition into a soft, conformable state when the processor reaches its operating temperature (typically between 45°C and 60°C).

Silicone-free PCMs reduce contact resistance through the following mechanisms:

  • Viscosity Reduction and Wetting: Once the processor heats up, the solid pad melts into a high-viscosity fluid. Under the clamping pressure of the heatsink, the melted material flows into the microscopic valleys of both the die and the heatsink, displacing all trapped air.
  • Ultra-Thin Bond Line: As the material changes phase and expands slightly, excess material is squeezed out until the metal peaks of the heatsink almost touch the silicon die. This minimizes the bond-line thickness, drastically shortening the path heat must travel.
  • Resistance to Pump-Out: Traditional thermal pastes can suffer from "pump-out," where repeated heating and cooling cycles cause the paste to squeeze out of the interface, leaving voids behind. When a processor cools down, a PCM solidifies again, locking it in place and preventing it from migrating out of the contact zone.
  • Elimination of Siloxane Outgassing: Standard silicone-based pads release low-molecular-weight siloxane gases under high heat. These gases can condense on nearby optical lenses or electrical contacts, creating insulating layers and reducing mechanical stability. Silicone-free PCMs use acrylic, polyolefin, or synthetic wax bases, ensuring stable, long-term thermal contact without contaminating sensitive electronics.

Liquid-Metal Compounds

Liquid-metal thermal compounds are typically eutectic alloys composed of gallium, indium, and tin (such as Galinstan). Unlike conventional pastes or pads that use polymer bases filled with ceramic or metal powders, liquid metal is 100% metal and remains in a liquid state at room temperature.

Liquid metal minimizes contact resistance more effectively than any other material due to several distinct physical properties:

  • Superior Bulk Thermal Conductivity: Standard thermal pastes and pads have thermal conductivities ranging from 1 to 15 W/m·K. Liquid-metal alloys boast thermal conductivities between 60 and 85 W/m·K. Because the material itself conducts heat nearly as well as the copper heatsink, the resistance across the interface drops dramatically.
  • True Liquid Flow: Because it contains no solid filler particles (like the aluminum oxide or zinc oxide found in pastes), there are no microscopic granules to hold the heatsink and processor apart. Liquid metal achieves a near-zero bond-line thickness, filling only the sub-micron crevices while allowing maximum direct peak-to-peak contact between the die and the heatsink.
  • Metallurgical Wetting: Under pressure, liquid metal exhibits high surface wetting on silicon and metals like copper and nickel. It forms an intimate, void-free boundary layer at the atomic level, virtually eliminating interfacial boundary resistance.
  • Thermal Stability: Unlike organic compounds that can dry out, crack, or degrade over time due to thermal cycling, liquid metal has an extremely high boiling point and does not evaporate. It maintains its fluid state and low contact resistance indefinitely, provided it is paired with compatible metals like nickel-plated copper to prevent alloy diffusion.
