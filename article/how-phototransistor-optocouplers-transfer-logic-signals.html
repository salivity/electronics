How Phototransistor Optocouplers Transfer Logic Signals

This article explains how phototransistor optocouplers transmit digital logic signals between isolated circuits while blocking dangerous common-mode voltages. You will learn about the internal components of an optocoupler, the step-by-step process of optical signal transmission, the physical structure of the insulating barrier, and how these devices maintain signal integrity in noisy, high-voltage electronic environments.

The Internal Architecture

A phototransistor optocoupler (or optoisolator) contains two primary elements housed within a single, opaque package:

  • The Emitter: An infrared light-emitting diode (LED) on the input side.
  • The Detector: A photosensitive silicon transistor on the output side.

Between the LED and the phototransistor lies a microscopic insulating gap made of a transparent dielectric material, such as glass, air, or clear silicone plastic. There is zero conductive metal connecting the input pins to the output pins.

Transferring the Logic Signal

To transfer a logic signal without a physical wire, the optocoupler converts electrical energy into light, and then back into electrical energy.

  1. Electrical Input: A digital logic high signal (such as 3.3V or 5V from a microcontroller) is applied to the input pins. This causes current to flow through the internal infrared LED.
  2. Optical Transmission: The current energizes the LED, causing it to emit infrared light across the transparent insulating gap. A logic low signal turns the LED off, stopping the light.
  3. Photoconversion: The emitted photons strike the base region of the internal phototransistor on the other side of the gap.
  4. Electrical Output: The absorbed light generates electron-hole pairs in the phototransistor's base, acting just like a standard base current. This switches the transistor on, allowing current to flow between the collector and emitter on the output side.

By using a pull-up or pull-down resistor on the output side, the switching of the phototransistor recreates the original digital logic signal in the secondary circuit.

Blocking High Common-Mode Voltages

Common-mode voltage is a voltage potential that appears equally on both signal lines of a circuit relative to a local ground. In industrial motors, solar inverters, and medical equipment, one part of the system may operate at hundreds or thousands of volts higher than the sensitive logic controller. Connecting them directly with copper wire would instantly destroy the logic controller.

Phototransistor optocouplers block these high common-mode voltages through two main mechanisms:

  • High Dielectric Strength: The transparent material separating the LED and phototransistor acts as a physical voltage barrier. While photons pass through effortlessly, electrical current cannot. Depending on the thickness and material of this barrier, optocouplers can easily block anywhere from 1,000V to over 5,000V of continuous or surge voltage.
  • Elimination of Ground Loops: Because the input LED and output phototransistor do not share a common ground path, current has no return path between the two sides. The input circuit references its own local ground, and the output circuit references a completely separate ground.

Overcoming Parasitic Capacitance (CMTI)

While DC voltages are completely blocked by the dielectric gap, rapidly changing AC common-mode voltages (transients) present a unique challenge. The physical proximity of the LED lead frame and the phototransistor lead frame creates a tiny, unintended "parasitic" capacitance across the gap—typically around 0.5 to 2 picofarads.

If the common-mode voltage spikes rapidly (high \(dV/dt\)), this capacitance can allow displacement current to jump the gap, falsely triggering the phototransistor and corrupting the logic signal.

To block these rapid voltage transients, high-performance optocouplers include an internal Faraday shield. This is a microscopic, optically transparent, conductive layer placed directly between the LED and the phototransistor. This shield shunts capacitive noise currents safely to the output ground before they can reach the base of the phototransistor. This capability is measured as Common-Mode Transient Immunity (CMTI), ensuring that even if the voltage across the barrier swings by thousands of volts in a microsecond, the logic signal transfers without error.
