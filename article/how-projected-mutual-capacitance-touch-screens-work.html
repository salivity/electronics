How Projected Mutual-Capacitance Touch Screens Work

This article explains the underlying technology of projected mutual-capacitance touch screens and how they accurately detect multiple simultaneous finger touches through a protective glass overlay. By utilizing a microscopic grid of conductive electrodes, these displays generate an electrostatic field that projects directly through insulating glass. When one or more fingers approach the screen, they disrupt this field at specific intersections, allowing an internal controller to measure capacitance drops and calculate precise multi-touch coordinates in real time.

The Electrode Grid Architecture

At the core of a projected mutual-capacitance touch screen is a two-layer grid of microscopic conductive lines, typically made from Indium Tin Oxide (ITO). ITO is used because it is both electrically conductive and optically transparent, allowing light from the display beneath to pass through unhindered.

The grid is divided into two distinct layers separated by a thin insulating material:

  • Transmit (Tx) Electrodes: Arranged in rows (or columns), these lines are driven by an alternating current (AC) voltage signal from the touch controller.
  • Receive (Rx) Electrodes: Arranged at a 90-degree angle to the Tx lines, these lines measure the electrical charge coupled from the transmitting layer.

Every intersection where a Tx line crosses an Rx line forms a unique capacitor. A standard smartphone screen contains hundreds or thousands of these miniature capacitive nodes.

Projecting the Field Through Glass

For the electronics to remain safe from moisture, dust, and physical damage, the ITO electrode grid is bonded beneath a protective glass overlay, such as chemically strengthened aluminosilicate glass.

When the AC signal is applied to the Tx electrodes, an electrostatic field is generated between the Tx and Rx intersections. Rather than staying strictly confined between the two microscopic layers, the "fringing" lines of this electric field project upward and outward. Because glass is a dielectric material (an electrical insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field), it allows these fringing electrostatic fields to pass right through to the top surface of the screen.

How a Finger Alters Mutual Capacitance

Each intersection on the grid holds a baseline amount of electrical charge, known as its mutual capacitance. The system constantly monitors this baseline.

The human body is filled with water and electrolytes, making a finger an effective electrical conductor that acts as a virtual ground. When a finger touches the protective glass overlay, it enters the projected fringing electric field. Because the finger is conductive, it steals a tiny portion of the electrical charge that was previously flowing from the Tx electrode to the Rx electrode.

Instead of the full charge reaching the Rx line, a fraction of the current is shunted through the finger and into the human body. This interaction causes a measurable drop in the mutual capacitance at that specific intersection.

Detecting Multiple Simultaneous Touches

Older capacitive technologies, such as self-capacitance, measure the capacitance of an entire row or column at once. If two fingers touch a self-capacitance screen simultaneously, the controller cannot determine which specific intersections are being pressed, resulting in false positional readings known as "ghosting."

Mutual-capacitance eliminates this problem by addressing each intersection individually. The touch controller operates using the following sequence:

  1. Sequential Driving: The controller sends an AC signal down a single Tx line at a time, cycling through all the Tx lines in milliseconds.
  2. Simultaneous Reading: As one Tx line is active, the controller measures the voltage at every single Rx line crossing that active Tx line.
  3. Heat Map Generation: By repeating this process across the entire grid hundreds of times per second, the controller builds a complete two-dimensional map of capacitance values across the screen.

Because every single intersection is measured independently, the system can detect a capacitance drop at any number of nodes at the exact same time. When multiple fingers touch the glass, the controller sees multiple isolated pockets of reduced capacitance on its 2D map. Advanced algorithms then calculate the center point (centroid) of each pocket, translating those raw electrical measurements into precise X and Y coordinates for the device's operating system to execute multi-touch gestures like pinching, zooming, and rotating.
