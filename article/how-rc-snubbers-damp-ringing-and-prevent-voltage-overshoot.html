How RC Snubbers Damp Ringing and Prevent Voltage Overshoot

Resistor-capacitor (RC) snubber networks are essential passive circuits used in power electronics to suppress high-frequency parasitic ringing and prevent destructive voltage overshoot across switching devices. This article provides a straightforward explanation of the causes behind voltage transients in switching circuits, the fundamental physics of how RC snubbers dissipate excess energy to damp these oscillations, and the standard methodology for selecting the right resistor and capacitor values to protect power switches like MOSFETs and IGBTs.

The Cause of Parasitic Ringing and Voltage Overshoot

In an ideal switching circuit, transistors turn on and off instantaneously without loss or oscillation. In reality, physical circuit boards and components contain unavoidable parasitic elements:

  • Parasitic Inductance (\(L_p\)): Found in PCB traces, component leads, and transformer leakage paths.
  • Parasitic Capacitance (\(C_p\)): Exists within the semiconductor switch itself (such as the output capacitance, \(C_{oss}\), of a MOSFET) and between circuit board layers.

When a power switch rapidly turns off, the current flowing through the parasitic inductance is abruptly interrupted. According to Faraday's law of induction (\(V = L \cdot di/dt\)), a rapid change in current (\(di/dt\)) across an inductor generates a massive voltage spike. This initial spike is known as voltage overshoot.

Immediately following the overshoot, the energy trapped in the parasitic inductor begins to transfer back and forth with the parasitic capacitor. This creates an unintentional resonant tank circuit (LC circuit) that oscillates at a specific high frequency, defined by the formula:

\[f_{ring} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_p C_p}}\]

Because the parasitic resistance in the circuit path is typically very low, there is nothing to absorb this bouncing energy. The result is sustained high-frequency voltage oscillations known as parasitic ringing.

Why Overshoot and Ringing Are Dangerous

Leaving parasitic ringing and overshoot unaddressed leads to immediate and long-term failures in electronic systems:

  1. Avalanche Breakdown: If the voltage overshoot exceeds the maximum breakdown voltage rating (\(V_{DS(max)}\) or \(V_{CE(max)}\)) of the power switch, the device can enter avalanche breakdown and suffer immediate thermal destruction.
  2. Electromagnetic Interference (EMI): High-frequency ringing acts like a broadcast antenna on PCB traces, radiating electromagnetic noise that can disrupt nearby logic circuits and cause the device to fail regulatory EMI compliance testing.
  3. Increased Switching Losses: Oscillations during state transitions increase the stress and heat generated within the semiconductor, reducing overall system efficiency and lifespan.

How the RC Snubber Network Works

An RC snubber network consists of a resistor (\(R_s\)) and a capacitor (\(C_s\)) connected in series, placed directly in parallel across the power switch (e.g., from Drain to Source on a MOSFET) or across the inductive load. It protects the switch through two primary mechanisms: controlling the rate of voltage change and damping the resonant tank.

1. Clamping Voltage Overshoot (The Capacitor's Role)

At high frequencies, a capacitor acts as a low-impedance path. When the switch turns off and the inductor attempts to generate a sudden voltage spike, the snubber capacitor (\(C_s\)) absorbs the surge current. Because the voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously (\(I = C \cdot dv/dt\)), the capacitor slows down the rate of voltage rise (\(dv/dt\)). By providing an alternate path for the current during the turn-off transition, the peak voltage overshoot across the switch is significantly reduced.

2. Damping Parasitic Ringing (The Resistor's Role)

Adding a capacitor alone does not stop ringing; it simply lowers the resonant frequency by increasing the total capacitance in the LC tank. To stop the oscillation, energy must be removed from the system.

This is the role of the snubber resistor (\(R_s\)). As the high-frequency ringing current flows into and out of the snubber capacitor, it is forced to pass through the series resistor. The resistor converts this oscillating electrical energy into heat, rapidly reducing the amplitude of the waveform until it flatlines.

In physics terms, the resistor lowers the Quality Factor (\(Q\)) of the parasitic LC circuit. When the resistance is matched to the characteristic impedance of the parasitic tank, the circuit becomes critically damped, eliminating ringing entirely within a single cycle.

How to Design and Size an RC Snubber

Designing an effective RC snubber requires measuring the existing circuit behavior and calculating the appropriate component values.

Step 1: Measure the Ringing Frequency

Using an oscilloscope with a low-inductance probe (such as a ground spring tip), measure the frequency of the parasitic ringing (\(f_{ring1}\)) across the switch during turn-off.

Step 2: Determine Parasitic Inductance and Capacitance

Solder a known film or ceramic capacitor (\(C_{test}\)) directly across the switch until the ringing frequency drops to exactly half of its original value (\(f_{ring2} = f_{ring1} / 2\)). Because frequency is inversely proportional to the square root of capacitance, halving the frequency means the total capacitance has quadrupled. Therefore, the original parasitic capacitance is:

\[C_p = \frac{C_{test}}{3}\]

Once \(C_p\) and \(f_{ring1}\) are known, calculate the parasitic inductance (\(L_p\)):

\[L_p = \frac{1}{(2\pi f_{ring1})^2 \cdot C_p}\]

Step 3: Calculate the Snubber Resistor (\(R_s\))

To damp the ringing effectively, the snubber resistor should be equal to the characteristic impedance (\(Z_0\)) of the parasitic tank circuit:

\[R_s = Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L_p}{C_p}}\]

Step 4: Calculate the Snubber Capacitor (\(C_s\))

The snubber capacitor must be large enough to allow the resistor to act at the ringing frequency, but small enough to prevent excessive power loss at the main switching frequency. A standard rule of thumb is to choose a capacitance between 1 to 4 times the value of the parasitic capacitance:

\[C_s \approx 3 \cdot C_p\]

Step 5: Calculate Resistor Power Dissipation

Every time the switch turns on and off, the snubber capacitor charges to the supply voltage (\(V\)) and discharges completely through the snubber resistor. The power dissipated (\(P_R\)) as heat in the resistor depends only on the capacitance, the voltage, and the switching frequency (\(f_{sw}\)) of the power supply—not the resistance value itself:

\[P_R = C_s \cdot V^2 \cdot f_{sw}\]

Always select a resistor with a power rating at least twice the calculated \(P_R\) value to ensure thermal reliability. Additionally, use low-inductance resistors (such as metal film or carbon composition) and stable, low-ESR capacitors (such as C0G/NP0 ceramics or polypropylene film) so the snubber components do not introduce new parasitics into the circuit.
