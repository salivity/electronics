How Reverse Bias Widens the Depletion Region

Applying a reverse bias to a semiconductor p-n junction widens the insulating depletion region by using an external voltage to pull mobile charge carriers away from the junction interface. By connecting the positive terminal of a power source to the n-type material and the negative terminal to the p-type material, electrons and holes are drawn toward the battery terminals. This movement leaves behind a wider zone of uncovered, immobile ions, which increases the internal electric field and creates a strong insulating barrier that prevents standard current flow.

The P-N Junction at Equilibrium

To understand reverse bias, you must first understand the p-n junction without an external voltage applied:

  • P-type semiconductor: Contains an abundance of positively charged "holes" (spaces where an electron is missing).
  • N-type semiconductor: Contains an abundance of negatively charged free electrons.

When these two materials are joined, electrons near the border naturally diffuse across the junction into the p-side to fill the holes. When an electron leaves the n-side, it leaves behind a positively charged, immobile donor ion. When that electron fills a hole on the p-side, it creates a negatively charged, immobile acceptor ion.

This transfer creates a thin zone on both sides of the border that is stripped of free, mobile carriers. This is the depletion region. The immobile ions within this zone create a built-in electric field that eventually stops further electrons and holes from crossing, putting the semiconductor in a state of equilibrium.

Applying the Reverse Bias

Reverse bias occurs when an external voltage source (like a battery) is connected to the p-n junction in a specific polarity:

  1. The positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-type material.
  2. The negative terminal of the battery is connected to the p-type material.

The Widening Mechanism

Once the reverse bias voltage is applied, electrostatic attraction immediately alters the positioning of the mobile charge carriers:

  • On the N-side: The positive terminal of the battery attracts the negatively charged free electrons. These electrons are pulled away from the p-n junction and toward the battery terminal.
  • On the P-side: The negative terminal of the battery attracts the positively charged holes. These holes are also pulled away from the p-n junction and toward the opposite battery terminal.

As these mobile electrons and holes retreat from the center, they uncover more immobile positive ions on the n-side and more immobile negative ions on the p-side. Because the depletion region is defined as the area devoid of mobile charge carriers, uncovering more fixed ions directly translates to a physically wider depletion region.

The Insulating Effect

As the depletion region widens, it acts as a stronger insulator. The newly uncovered ions add to the strength of the existing built-in electric field. The depletion region will continue to widen until the internal electric field is equal in magnitude to the applied external voltage of the battery.

At this point, the potential barrier at the junction is too high for majority carriers (electrons in the n-side, holes in the p-side) to overcome. With no mobile carriers left in the central zone to conduct electricity, the widened depletion region effectively blocks current flow across the semiconductor. (Only a minuscule, negligible "leakage current" caused by thermally generated minority carriers can pass through).

Practical Applications in Electronics

Engineers intentionally manipulate the width of the depletion region using reverse bias for several electronic components:

  • Rectifier Diodes: Used to convert alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC). The widened depletion region during the reverse-bias cycle acts as an open switch, blocking current from flowing backward into a circuit.
  • Varactor Diodes: A p-n junction acts like a capacitor, where the p-side and n-side are the conductive plates, and the insulating depletion region is the dielectric between them. By changing the reverse bias voltage, engineers can change the width of the depletion region, which alters the capacitance. This is commonly used in radio frequency (RF) tuning circuits.
  • Photodiodes: A wider depletion region provides a larger surface area to absorb incoming light (photons). Photodiodes are operated in reverse bias so that when light hits the wide depletion region, it generates electron-hole pairs that are quickly swept across the junction, creating a measurable electrical current used in light sensors and fiber optics.
