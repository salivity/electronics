How Right to Repair Laws Force Tech Companies to Share Parts

Newly enacted right-to-repair legislation is transforming the consumer electronics industry by legally requiring manufacturers to make repair resources available to the general public and independent repair shops. Under these new mandates, tech companies can no longer monopolize the repair process by withholding critical components and information. Instead, they are compelled to provide board-level schematics, proprietary diagnostic software, and genuine spare parts at fair market prices. This article breaks down the specific mechanisms of these laws, how they force compliance, and the resulting impact on parts availability, software access, and technical documentation.

Mandating Access to Board Schematics and Documentation

Historically, manufacturers treated circuit board schematics, wiring diagrams, and service manuals as trade secrets. Without these blueprints, independent technicians had to rely on trial and error or leaked documents to fix complex motherboard issues.

New right-to-repair laws directly challenge this practice. Legislation passed in major markets, such as New York, California, Minnesota, and the European Union, explicitly requires Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) to provide the same documentation to independent repair providers and consumers that they provide to their own authorized service networks.

These laws stipulate that documentation must be provided free of charge or at a minimal cost covering only the physical distribution. While companies are still permitted to protect genuine trade secrets, the legislation clarifies that standard repair manuals, component layouts, and troubleshooting guides do not qualify for this exemption. If an authorized technician has a schematic to diagnose a short circuit, the public must have access to it as well.

Unlocking Proprietary Diagnostic Software

Modern electronics are heavily reliant on software not just to function, but to be repaired. Manufacturers frequently use diagnostic tools to identify hardware faults and employ "parts pairing"—a software lock that prevents a device from functioning fully unless a replacement part's serial number is cryptographically paired to the main board by the manufacturer's server.

Right-to-repair laws dismantle these software barriers in two ways:

  1. Equal Access to Diagnostics: Manufacturers must make their diagnostic and calibration software available to the public. This includes software updates required to reset error codes or calibrate new screens and batteries after installation.
  2. Bans on Parts Pairing: The newest wave of legislation, such as laws recently passed in Oregon and Colorado, includes specific provisions outlawing the use of parts pairing to hinder repairs. Manufacturers can no longer use software to disable features (like biometric scanners or battery health indicators) simply because a third-party or harvested genuine part was installed without the manufacturer's digital authorization.

Enforcing the Supply of Genuine Spare Parts

Before these laws, independent repair shops were often forced to buy gray-market, counterfeit, or salvaged parts because OEMs refused to sell to them. Right-to-repair legislation compels manufacturers to open their supply chains.

Under these new legal frameworks, companies must sell replacement parts and specialized tools to consumers and independent technicians on "fair and reasonable" terms. The laws define "fair and reasonable" by comparing the price and terms offered to the public against those offered to Authorized Service Providers (ASPs). Manufacturers are legally prohibited from artificially inflating the price of a replacement screen or battery to make independent repair economically unviable compared to buying a new device or using an authorized repair center.

Furthermore, Laws like California’s SB 244 dictate the duration of this supply. For electronics priced between $50 and $99.99, manufacturers must provide parts and documentation for at least three years after the last date the product was manufactured. For devices costing $100 or more, that requirement extends to seven years.

Legal Penalties Driving Manufacturer Compliance

Manufacturers are complying with these mandates because the financial and operational penalties for non-compliance are severe. Right-to-repair laws are typically enforced by state Attorneys General or national consumer protection agencies.

Violations are treated as unlawful business practices, carrying civil penalties that range from $1,000 to $5,000 per violation per day. Because it is logistically inefficient for global tech companies to create one repair ecosystem for states like California and New York and a restricted ecosystem for the rest of the country, these regional laws effectively force nationwide—and often global—compliance. Rather than face millions of dollars in fines and fragmented supply chains, major manufacturers have launched self-service repair portals to sell parts, rent tools, and distribute manuals to the public.
