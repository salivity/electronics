How RS-485 Balanced Differential Signaling Reaches 1 Kilometer

This article provides a concise overview of how RS-485 achieves long-distance data transmission in harsh industrial environments. By utilizing balanced differential signaling over twisted-pair cabling, RS-485 cancels out electromagnetic interference, compensates for ground potential differences, and supports multi-drop networks spanning up to 1.2 kilometers. Below, we examine the exact electrical mechanisms that make this long-range communication possible.

The Principle of Balanced Differential Signaling

Standard single-ended communication protocols, like RS-232, transmit data using a single wire referenced to a common ground. Over long distances, voltage drops and electrical noise corrupt this signal.

RS-485 solves this by using balanced differential signaling. Instead of one data wire, it uses two wires—commonly labeled A and B. The transmitter sends the original signal down one wire and an exact inverted (opposite) copy of the signal down the other.

The receiver does not measure the voltage of the wires relative to the ground. Instead, it measures the voltage difference between wire A and wire B. If the voltage on A is higher than B by at least 200 millivolts, it registers as one binary state. If B is higher than A by the same margin, it registers as the opposite binary state.

Noise Cancellation and Common-Mode Rejection

Industrial environments are filled with heavy machinery, motors, and high-voltage lines that generate severe electromagnetic interference (EMI). Over a kilometer of cable, this interference easily induces unwanted voltages into communication lines.

RS-485 relies on twisted-pair cabling to neutralize this threat. Because the two wires are twisted tightly together, any external electromagnetic noise affects both wires equally. This is known as common-mode noise.

When the receiver subtracts the voltage of wire B from wire A to read the data, the identical noise present on both wires is mathematically canceled out. This capability, known as Common-Mode Rejection, keeps the data signal clean even when the cable runs alongside high-voltage industrial equipment for over a kilometer.

Overcoming Ground Potential Differences

When connecting multiple devices across a large factory floor, the electrical ground at one end of a 1,000-meter cable will rarely match the electrical ground at the other end. In single-ended systems, this ground potential difference causes massive data corruption and can damage hardware.

Because RS-485 receivers only care about the difference in voltage between the two signal wires, they are largely immune to shifts in ground potential. The RS-485 standard specifies a common-mode voltage range of -7V to +12V, allowing the network to function reliably even when ground voltages fluctuate across long distances.

High Receiver Sensitivity and Signal Attenuation

As an electrical signal travels down a kilometer of copper wire, the resistance and capacitance of the cable cause the signal to weaken—a process called attenuation.

A typical RS-485 transmitter outputs a differential voltage of at least 1.5 volts. By the time that signal travels 1,200 meters, it may degrade significantly. However, RS-485 receivers are designed with high sensitivity, requiring a differential of only 200 millivolts (0.2 volts) to accurately detect a valid logic state. This wide margin allows the signal to lose over 85% of its strength over a long cable run and still be read with zero data loss.

Multi-Drop Topology and Line Termination

RS-485 is designed as a multi-drop bus, meaning up to 32 standard "unit loads" (or up to 256 devices using modern high-impedance transceivers) can be connected in a daisy-chain configuration along a single kilometer-long cable.

To maintain signal integrity over this distance with multiple connected devices, RS-485 networks use termination resistors—typically 120 ohms—placed at both extreme ends of the cable. These resistors match the characteristic impedance of the twisted-pair wire. Proper termination absorbs the signal at the ends of the line, preventing it from bouncing back and causing data-corrupting signal reflections.

Finally, distance and data rate are inversely proportional in RS-485 networks. While short RS-485 cables can handle speeds up to 10 Mbps, spanning the maximum distance of 1.2 kilometers requires lowering the baud rate to roughly 100 kbps or less, ensuring the widened signal pulses remain distinct and readable at the far end of the line.
