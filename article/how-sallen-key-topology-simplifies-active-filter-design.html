How Sallen-Key Topology Simplifies Active Filter Design

This article explores how the Sallen-Key topology streamlines the engineering of second-order active low-pass and high-pass filters in electronics. By examining its reliance on minimal components, high input impedance, and modular cascading capabilities, this guide explains why this circuit configuration remains an industry standard for signal processing. Readers will quickly understand the structural benefits of the Sallen-Key design and how it eliminates the complex math and bulky components associated with traditional passive filters.

The Sallen-Key topology, introduced by R.P. Sallen and E.L. Key in 1955, is an electronic filter configuration used to implement second-order active filters. A second-order filter decreases or increases signal frequencies beyond a cutoff point at a rate of 40 decibels per decade (or 12 decibels per octave).

Elimination of Inductors

Traditional passive second-order filters require inductors, resistors, and capacitors (RLC circuits) to achieve the necessary phase shift and frequency roll-off. Inductors are bulky, expensive, prone to electromagnetic interference, and difficult to manufacture with high precision.

The Sallen-Key topology eliminates the need for inductors entirely. Instead, it uses an operational amplifier (op-amp) combined with just two resistors and two capacitors. The active feedback loop of the op-amp simulates the mathematical behavior of an inductor, drastically reducing the physical size, weight, and cost of the circuit.

Minimal Component Count and Unity Gain

One of the primary ways the Sallen-Key design simplifies engineering is through its minimal component count. In its most basic form—the unity-gain configuration—the op-amp is wired as a simple voltage follower.

This approach offers several immediate design advantages:

  • Fewer parts: A unity-gain Sallen-Key filter requires only one op-amp, two resistors, and two capacitors.
  • No gain-setting resistors: Because the op-amp's output is tied directly to its inverting input, designers do not need to calculate or source precision resistors to set the amplifier's gain.
  • High accuracy: Unity-gain configurations are exceptionally stable and less susceptible to component tolerance variations, ensuring the filter performs predictably in real-world applications.

Easy Conversion Between Low-Pass and High-Pass

The physical layout of the Sallen-Key topology makes switching between low-pass and high-pass configurations effortless. The core architecture remains identical for both filter types; only the positions of the resistors and capacitors are swapped.

  • Low-Pass Configuration: The two resistors are placed in series along the main signal path leading to the non-inverting input of the op-amp, while the two capacitors provide the feedback and grounding paths.
  • High-Pass Configuration: The two capacitors are placed in series along the main signal path, and the two resistors provide the feedback and grounding paths.

Because the underlying math remains symmetrical, engineers can use the exact same transfer function equations to calculate the cutoff frequency (\(f_c\)) and quality factor (\(Q\)) for both filter types. The cutoff frequency for both configurations is determined by the simple formula:

\(f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{R_1 R_2 C_1 C_2}}\)

By choosing equal value resistors (\(R_1 = R_2 = R\)) and equal value capacitors (\(C_1 = C_2 = C\)), the math simplifies even further to:

\(f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}\)

High Input Impedance and Easy Cascading

In passive filter design, connecting two filter stages together causes "loading," where the second stage draws current from the first, altering the cutoff frequency of the entire system. Complex impedance-matching calculations are required to prevent this.

The Sallen-Key topology resolves this through the inherent properties of the op-amp. Because the signal enters the non-inverting input of the op-amp, the filter possesses very high input impedance and very low output impedance. This isolates the filter stage from whatever precedes or follows it.

Consequently, engineers can design fourth-order, sixth-order, or higher-order filters (such as Butterworth, Chebyshev, or Bessel filters) simply by placing multiple Sallen-Key stages in series. Each stage operates independently, allowing designers to break down complex, high-order filter math into manageable second-order segments without worrying about inter-stage loading effects.
