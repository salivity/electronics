How Semiconductor Curve Tracers Plot Transistor Family Curves

This article explains the operational mechanics of semiconductor curve tracers in failure-analysis electronics. Specifically, it details how these instruments simultaneously sweep collector voltage and step base current to generate characteristic transistor family curves. Finally, it examines how engineers interpret these visual plots to pinpoint specific silicon degradation, leakage pathways, and catastrophic junction failures.

The Mechanics of the Collector Voltage Sweep

To map the output characteristics of a bipolar junction transistor (BJT), a curve tracer must evaluate how collector current (\(I_C\)) responds to changes in collector-emitter voltage (\(V_{CE}\)). The instrument achieves this through a collector supply circuit that generates a continuously varying voltage sweep, typically takes the form of a rectified sine wave or a precision sawtooth waveform.

This voltage is applied directly between the collector and emitter terminals of the Device Under Test (DUT). The voltage starts at zero, increases to a user-defined peak value, and returns to zero. In the curve tracer's display system, this sweeping \(V_{CE}\) is routed to the horizontal (X-axis) amplifier. Simultaneously, a current-sensing resistor in series with the collector measures the resulting \(I_C\), routing this value to the vertical (Y-axis) amplifier. A single sweep of the collector voltage produces one trace on the screen, representing the transistor's behavior under a single, static input condition.

Stepping the Base Current

A single trace is insufficient for comprehensive characterization; engineers need to see how the transistor behaves across its entire operational range. To achieve this, the curve tracer uses a staircase generator to supply a stepped current (\(I_B\)) to the base terminal of the transistor.

Instead of holding the base current constant, the instrument injects a precise, discrete level of current into the base for the duration of one complete collector voltage sweep. Once the collector voltage sweep returns to zero, the staircase generator increments the base current to the next predefined step. This synchronization is critical: the base current steps up only when the collector voltage is at zero, preventing thermal shock or transient switching damage to the DUT.

Generating the Family of Curves

When the collector sweep and base step generators operate in tandem, they create a "family" of curves. For example, if the instrument is configured for five steps of base current, the collector voltage will sweep from zero to its peak five separate times.

During the first sweep, the base current is at step one, drawing the lowest trace on the display. During the second sweep, the base current increments to step two, causing the transistor to conduct more heavily and drawing a second trace higher up on the vertical \(I_C\) axis. Because this cycle repeats rapidly—often tied to the 50 Hz or 60 Hz mains frequency or a fast internal oscillator—the persistence of the display (or digital memory buffer) makes all the traces appear simultaneously. The result is a complete map displaying the saturation region, the active linear region, and the cutoff region of the transistor.

Application in Failure-Analysis Electronics

In failure analysis, comparing a suspect transistor's family of curves against a known-good specification quickly reveals physical defects inside the semiconductor package:

  • Open Bonds or Cracked Die: If the collector voltage sweeps across the X-axis but no vertical deflection occurs regardless of the base current step, the collector or base path has failed open.
  • Collector-Emitter Shorts: If the trace shoots vertically up the Y-axis immediately as \(V_{CE}\) leaves zero—independent of the base steps—the junction is shorted, behaving like a low-value resistor.
  • Degraded Gain (\(h_{FE}\)): The vertical spacing between the stepped traces represents the transistor's current gain. If the lines are compressed closely together or vary wildly in spacing, the silicon has suffered thermal degradation or contamination, resulting in nonlinear gain.
  • Junction Leakage (\(I_{CEO}\)): By observing the bottom-most trace (where base current is stepped to zero), an analyst can measure leakage. If this baseline trace slopes upward or sits elevated above the zero-current axis, the transistor is leaking current between the collector and emitter.
  • Soft Breakdown: At the far right of the X-axis (high \(V_{CE}\)), the traces should remain relatively flat until they hit the avalanche breakdown voltage, where they turn sharply upward. If the curves begin curving upward prematurely with a gradual slope, the device is suffering from "soft breakdown," often caused by surface contamination or channeling defects in the silicon.
