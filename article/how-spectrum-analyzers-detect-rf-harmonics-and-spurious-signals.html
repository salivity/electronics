How Spectrum Analyzers Detect RF Harmonics and Spurious Signals

This article explains how swept-tuned and real-time spectrum analyzers identify, measure, and display unwanted harmonics and spurious emissions in the frequency domain for radio frequency (RF) electronics. It covers the fundamental operational differences between the two analyzer types, detailing how swept-tuned instruments excel at wide-band static measurements while real-time analyzers capture elusive, transient anomalies.

Understanding Harmonics and Spurious Emissions

In RF electronics, non-linear components like amplifiers and mixers inevitably generate unwanted signals alongside the intended carrier frequency. These anomalies are categorized into two main types:

  • Harmonics: Signals that occur at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency (e.g., a 1 GHz signal producing harmonics at 2 GHz, 3 GHz, and 4 GHz). Because they are mathematically related to the fundamental signal, their locations in the frequency domain are predictable.
  • Spurious Emissions (Spurs): Unwanted signals that are not harmonically related to the fundamental frequency. They often result from internal clock leakage, power supply noise, or intermodulation products. Spurs can be continuous, drifting, or transient, making them much harder to locate.

Spectrum analyzers translate time-domain RF signals into the frequency domain, plotting amplitude against frequency to make these anomalies visible.

How Swept-Tuned Spectrum Analyzers Work

Traditional swept-tuned spectrum analyzers use a superheterodyne architecture. They operate by sweeping a local oscillator (LO) across a specified frequency range. As the LO sweeps, it mixes with the incoming RF signal, translating it to an intermediate frequency (IF) that passes through a Resolution Bandwidth (RBW) filter before reaching a detector.

Revealing Harmonics with Swept-Tuned Analyzers

Swept-tuned analyzers are ideal for measuring continuous, stable signals like harmonics. Because harmonics are static in frequency, the analyzer can sweep across a massive frequency span (often up to tens of gigahertz) and capture each harmonic sequentially as the LO tunes past them.

To reveal low-level harmonics and spurs using a swept-tuned analyzer, engineers adjust the RBW filter. Narrowing the RBW reduces the instrument's noise floor (Displayed Average Noise Level, or DANL), allowing weak spurious signals that were previously hidden in the noise to become visible.

The Limitation: Blind Time

The primary drawback of a swept-tuned analyzer is that it can only look at one narrow slice of frequency at any given microsecond. Furthermore, narrowing the RBW to see weak signals drastically increases the sweep time. If a spurious emission is transient—meaning it hops frequencies or only appears for a fraction of a second—it is highly likely to occur at a frequency the analyzer is not currently sweeping, rendering the spur invisible.

How Real-Time Spectrum Analyzers (RTSA) Work

Real-time spectrum analyzers solve the blind-time problem of swept-tuned architectures. Instead of sweeping a filter across a span, an RTSA digitizes a wide block of RF spectrum simultaneously using a high-speed Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).

Once digitized, dedicated Digital Signal Processing (DSP) hardware performs millions of Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs) per second. By overlapping these FFT calculations in memory, the RTSA processes the entire real-time bandwidth continuously with zero blind time.

Revealing Transient Spurs with RTSAs

Because the RTSA captures every event within its real-time bandwidth simultaneously, it excels at revealing intermittent spurious emissions, frequency-hopping interference, and short-duration switching transients. RTSAs use specialized display modes to show these events in the frequency domain:

  • Persistence Displays: Similar to a digital phosphor oscilloscope, a persistence display uses color-grading to show how often a signal occupies a specific frequency and amplitude. A continuous harmonic will appear bright red, while a brief, microsecond-long spurious burst will appear in cool blue. This allows engineers to see transient spurs hiding directly beneath or adjacent to a strong carrier signal.
  • Spectrograms: This mode plots frequency on the X-axis, time on the Y-axis, and amplitude as color. It creates a historical waterfall view, allowing engineers to see exactly when a spurious emission occurred and whether it is drifting in frequency over time due to thermal changes in the RF circuit.
  • Frequency Mask Triggering (FMT): Engineers can draw a mask in the frequency domain around a known signal. If an unexpected spurious emission breaches this mask for even a few nanoseconds, the RTSA triggers and captures the event into memory for deep analysis.

Choosing the Right Approach for RF Electronics

In practical RF testing, both methods are frequently combined. Many modern real-time spectrum analyzers include a swept-tuned mode.

Engineers use the swept-tuned mode to perform wide-span regulatory compliance sweeps, measuring the exact amplitude of predictable 2nd, 3rd, and 4th harmonics across a broad spectrum with high dynamic range. They then switch to real-time mode, zooming in on specific frequency bands of interest to hunt down elusive, intermittent spurious emissions caused by digital control lines, phase-locked loop (PLL) settling times, or unstable amplification stages.
