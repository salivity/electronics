How Thermosonic Wire Bonding Connects IC Dies to Leadframes

Thermosonic wire bonding is the primary manufacturing technique used to create electrical connections between a silicon semiconductor die and the metal leadframe inside an integrated circuit (IC) package. This article provides a comprehensive overview of how this microscopic assembly process utilizes a precise combination of heat, ultrasonic energy, and mechanical force to fuse ultra-thin gold or copper wires from the die bond pads to the package leads. By examining the core components, the step-by-step bonding cycle, and the differences between gold and copper materials, this guide explains how internal IC connections are reliably formed.

The Three Pillars of Thermosonic Bonding

Thermosonic bonding achieves a solid-state metallurgical weld without melting the base materials completely. Instead, it relies on three forces working simultaneously:

  1. Thermal Energy (Heat): The IC substrate and leadframe are placed on a heated stage, typically maintained between 150°C and 250°C. This heat softens the metals and increases atomic mobility.
  2. Ultrasonic Energy: A transducer vibrates the bonding tool (capillary) at frequencies usually between 60 kHz and 140 kHz. This rapid vibration scrubs away surface oxides and creates friction at the atomic level, which softens the wire further.
  3. Mechanical Force (Pressure): The capillary presses the wire against the pad or leadframe, forcing the clean, heated, and vibrating metal surfaces together so their atoms interdiffuse and form a permanent bond.

The Step-by-Step Wire Bonding Process

Thermosonic bonding almost exclusively uses a technique called "ball-stitch" bonding. A specialized ceramic tool with a microscopic hole through its center, called a capillary, feeds the thin wire (typically 15 to 50 microns in diameter) through the following cycle:

1. Forming the Free Air Ball (FAB)

A small tail of wire protrudes from the bottom of the capillary. An electronic flame-off (EFO) wand generates a high-voltage spark near the wire tip. The heat from the spark melts the tip of the wire, and surface tension naturally pulls the molten metal into a perfectly round sphere known as a Free Air Ball (FAB).

2. The First Bond (Ball Bond on the Die)

The capillary lowers the FAB onto the aluminum or copper bond pad located on the silicon die. Downward pressure and ultrasonic vibrations are applied for a few milliseconds. The heated stage, combined with the ultrasonic scrubbing, deforms the sphere into a mashed shape and fuses the wire to the die pad.

3. Looping

Once the ball bond is secure, the capillary rises while feeding out more wire. The machine moves the capillary in a carefully programmed trajectory toward the leadframe. This creates a precise arch, or "loop," which prevents the wire from short-circuiting against the edge of the silicon die.

4. The Second Bond (Stitch Bond on the Leadframe)

The capillary lowers the wire directly onto the target finger of the metal leadframe. Because there is no ball at this stage, the flat face of the capillary presses the side of the wire directly into the leadframe using mechanical force and ultrasonic energy. This creates a wedge-like connection known as a stitch bond.

5. Wire Termination

After the stitch bond is formed, the capillary rises slightly, and wire clamps inside the machine pull the wire upward until it snaps just above the stitch bond. This leaves a small wire tail protruding from the capillary, ready for the EFO spark to initiate the next cycle.

Gold vs. Copper Wire Bonding

While the mechanical process remains the same, choosing between gold and copper wire changes the parameters of thermosonic bonding.

Gold Wire

Historically, gold has been the industry standard. It is highly conductive, does not oxidize in the open air, and is very soft. Because it is soft, it requires less mechanical force and ultrasonic energy to bond, reducing the risk of cracking the fragile silicon die underneath the bond pad.

Copper Wire

Due to the high cost of gold, the electronics industry largely transitioned to copper wire. Copper offers superior electrical and thermal conductivity and is significantly cheaper, but it presents two major manufacturing challenges:

  • Oxidation: Copper oxidizes instantly when exposed to heat and air. During the EFO spark stage, a forming gas (typically a mixture of 95% nitrogen and 5% hydrogen) must be blown over the wire tip to prevent oxidation while the Free Air Ball forms.
  • Hardness: Copper is much harder than gold. It requires higher ultrasonic energy and greater downward force to bond properly. Engineers must tightly control these parameters to prevent the harder copper ball from damaging the sensitive circuitry beneath the die bond pad.
