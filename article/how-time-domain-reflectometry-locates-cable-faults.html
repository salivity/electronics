How Time-Domain Reflectometry Locates Cable Faults

Time-Domain Reflectometry (TDR) is a diagnostic technique used in electronics to measure the integrity of cables and printed circuit board (PCB) traces by injecting a fast-rising step pulse down a conductor and analyzing the reflected signals. This article explains how TDR works by detailing the mechanics of signal injection, the physics behind impedance discontinuities, how different faults appear as reflections, and the mathematical formula used to calculate the exact physical location of a break or defect.

The Mechanics of the Fast Step Pulse

A Time-Domain Reflectometer contains two primary components: a fast step generator and a high-speed sampling oscilloscope. To test a transmission line, the generator injects an electrical step voltage with a very fast rise time down the cable or PCB trace.

The rise time of this pulse is critical because it determines the spatial resolution of the measurement. A faster rise time allows the instrument to distinguish between two closely spaced faults. As this step pulse travels down the conductor, it propagates at a specific speed determined by the insulating material surrounding the wire or trace.

Impedance Discontinuities and Signal Reflections

In an ideal transmission line, the characteristic impedance (often 50 or 75 ohms) remains uniform along its entire length. When the injected step pulse travels through a uniform line terminated with a matching load, the energy is entirely absorbed at the end, and no signal reflects back to the source.

However, if the pulse encounters a change in the characteristic impedance—known as an impedance discontinuity—a portion of the incident wave's energy is reflected back toward the TDR source. Discontinuities can be caused by physical damage, sharp bends in a trace, faulty connectors, water ingress, or complete breaks in the wire.

Interpreting the Reflected Waveforms

The sampling oscilloscope at the TDR source monitors the line continuously. It displays the sum of the injected incident voltage and any reflected voltage over time. The polarity and amplitude of the reflected wave tell the engineer the exact nature of the fault. This relationship is governed by the reflection coefficient (\(\rho\)), which is calculated using the transmission line's impedance (\(Z_0\)) and the impedance of the fault (\(Z_L\)):

\(\rho = (Z_L - Z_0) / (Z_L + Z_0)\)

By observing how the voltage changes on the TDR display, you can identify specific issues:

  • Open Circuit (Breaks): If a cable is cut or a PCB trace is broken, the impedance at the fault approaches infinity (\(Z_L = \infty\)). The reflection coefficient becomes +1. The pulse hits the break and reflects back completely in-phase, causing the voltage on the TDR display to double.
  • Short Circuit: If the conductor is shorted to ground, the impedance at the fault drops to zero (\(Z_L = 0\)). The reflection coefficient becomes -1. The pulse reflects back completely out-of-phase, canceling out the original step pulse and dropping the displayed voltage back to zero.
  • Capacitive Discontinuity: If the impedance is momentarily lower than the line's characteristic impedance (such as a wide spot on a PCB trace), the TDR display shows a temporary downward dip in voltage before returning to the baseline level.
  • Inductive Discontinuity: If the impedance is momentarily higher (such as a narrow neck in a trace or a poor solder joint), the TDR display shows a temporary upward spike before settling.

Calculating the Exact Distance to the Fault

Locating the physical position of the fault requires measuring the time it takes for the step pulse to travel to the discontinuity and reflect back to the source. Because electricity travels slower in a dielectric material than in a vacuum, you must know the cable or PCB material's Velocity of Propagation (\(V_p\)), which is expressed as a percentage of the speed of light (\(c\)).

Once the TDR measures the round-trip transit time (\(t\)), it calculates the distance (\(D\)) to the fault using the following formula:

\(D = (c \times V_p \times t) / 2\)

The result is divided by two because the measured time represents the round trip of the signal—both to the fault and back to the oscilloscope. By combining the time delay with the shape of the reflected waveform, TDR provides an exact physical map of the transmission line, allowing engineers to pinpoint and diagnose invisible breaks and defects within millimeters.
