How TMDS Encodes HDMI Video and Audio in Home Theater Systems

Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) is the core technology responsible for transmitting high-speed, uncompressed video and audio data across HDMI cables in modern home theater systems. This article explains how TMDS converts digital media into electrical signals, uses an advanced 8b/10b encoding algorithm to prevent signal degradation, and combines audio and video onto the same physical wires without causing electromagnetic interference.

The Basics of Differential Signaling

Before encoding the data, TMDS relies on a method called differential signaling to physically move information from a source device (like a Blu-ray player or gaming console) to a display.

Standard HDMI connections use four shielded twisted pairs of copper wire for TMDS: three data channels (typically corresponding to Red, Green, and Blue video components) and one clock channel.

In each twisted pair, one wire carries the original positive electrical signal, while the second wire carries an exact inverted (negative) copy of that signal. When these signals reach the television or AV receiver, the device measures the difference between the two wires. If external electromagnetic interference—such as noise from power cables—affects the HDMI cable, it impacts both wires equally. The receiving device simply subtracts the identical noise, leaving behind a clean, uninterrupted digital signal.

The Two-Stage 8b/10b Encoding Process

Sending billions of digital bits (1s and 0s) per second down a copper wire creates two major problems: high-frequency interference caused by rapid voltage changes (transitions from 1 to 0), and Direct Current (DC) bias caused by sending too many 1s or too many 0s in a row. TMDS solves this by converting standard 8-bit data into 10-bit code words before transmission.

This conversion happens in two distinct stages:

  1. Transition Minimization (8-bit to 9-bit): The transmitter analyzes the original 8 bits of data to count how many times the signal transitions between 0 and 1. To keep these transitions to a minimum, the system runs a mathematical operation (either XOR or XNOR) on the bits. A 9th bit is added to the sequence solely to tell the receiving TV which mathematical operation was used, allowing the TV to reverse the process.
  2. DC Balancing (9-bit to 10-bit): Over time, the cable must carry an equal number of 1s and 0s to maintain a neutral electrical charge. The transmitter checks the running tally of 1s and 0s. If there is an imbalance, it inverts the 8 data bits (turning 1s into 0s and vice versa). A 10th bit is then added to tell the receiver whether or not the data was inverted.

How Video is Encoded

During active video transmission, pixel data is fed into the TMDS transmitter. Each color channel (Red, Green, Blue, or YCbCr equivalents) is processed as an 8-bit value.

The TMDS encoder converts each 8-bit color value into the 10-bit code described above. These 10-bit codes are then serialized—lined up in single file—and sent down the three TMDS data channels at massive speeds, synchronized by the fourth TMDS clock channel.

How Audio Shares the Same Wires

A common misconception is that HDMI cables have separate wires just for audio. Instead, TMDS embeds digital audio directly into the video channels using a method called time-division multiplexing.

Video frames are not transmitted as a continuous, unbroken stream. Just like old analog televisions, digital video signals include "blanking intervals"—brief pauses between the end of one horizontal line of pixels and the beginning of the next (Horizontal Blanking), and between the end of one full frame and the start of the next (Vertical Blanking).

During these blanking intervals, when no video data is being sent, the TMDS transmitter switches modes. It enters the "Data Island Period."

In this period, uncompressed digital audio (such as PCM) or compressed surround sound formats (like Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio) are grouped into packets. Because audio is much more sensitive to single-bit errors than video (a bad video bit is a barely noticeable dead pixel for a millisecond; a bad audio bit is a loud pop in the speakers), TMDS uses a different encoding method called TERC4 (TMDS Error Reduction Coding 4-bit) during the blanking intervals. TERC4 translates 4-bit audio and auxiliary data into highly resilient 10-bit codes, packs them into the video blanking spaces, and sends them down the exact same three data channels used for the picture.

Decoding at the Display or Receiver

When the TMDS signals arrive at your AV receiver or television, the process runs in reverse. The receiver uses the TMDS clock channel to lock onto the timing of the incoming stream. It separates the active video pixels from the Data Island packets (audio). It reads the 9th and 10th bits of the video data to undo the inversion and mathematical operations, stripping away the encoding to reveal the original 8-bit video and audio streams, which are then sent to your screen and speakers.
