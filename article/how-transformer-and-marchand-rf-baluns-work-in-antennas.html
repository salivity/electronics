How Transformer and Marchand RF Baluns Work in Antennas

This article explores how radio frequency (RF) baluns convert balanced signals from dipole antennas into unbalanced signals for coaxial transmission lines in wireless electronics. It breaks down the fundamental conflict between balanced and unbalanced components, the resulting issue of common-mode currents, and the exact physical mechanisms used by both magnetic transformer baluns and high-frequency Marchand baluns to achieve seamless signal conversion and impedance matching.

The Conflict: Balanced Antennas vs. Unbalanced Coax

In wireless electronics, a dipole antenna is a balanced component. It consists of two symmetrical metal rods. For the antenna to radiate or receive properly, the alternating RF currents on both rods must be equal in magnitude and opposite in phase (180 degrees apart). Neither side of the antenna is connected to an electrical ground.

Conversely, a coaxial cable is an unbalanced transmission line. It carries the RF signal on an inner center conductor, which is surrounded by an outer metal shield connected to the system ground. The current travels down the center conductor and returns on the inside surface of the outer shield, keeping the electromagnetic fields completely contained inside the cable.

When you connect a balanced dipole directly to an unbalanced coaxial cable, a physical mismatch occurs. One side of the dipole connects to the center conductor, and the other connects to the grounded shield. Because RF currents travel on the very outer skin of conductors (a phenomenon known as the skin effect), the current returning on the inside of the coax shield spills over the edge and travels down the outside of the shield.

This unwanted flow is called a common-mode current. It turns the coaxial cable itself into a radiating antenna, which distorts the dipole’s radiation pattern, causes signal loss, and introduces RF interference into the connected wireless circuitry. A balun (Balanced-to-Unbalanced) solves this problem by isolating the ground and forcing the currents to remain symmetrical.

How Transformer RF Baluns Work

Transformer baluns, often called magnetic or flux-coupled baluns, use electromagnetic induction to bridge the balanced and unbalanced domains. They are typically used in lower to mid-range RF frequencies (from a few megahertz up to a few gigahertz).

A standard voltage transformer balun consists of two wire windings wrapped around a magnetic ferrite core:

  1. The Unbalanced Side (Primary Winding): One end of this winding connects to the coaxial center conductor, and the other end connects directly to the coaxial ground shield.
  2. The Balanced Side (Secondary Winding): The two ends of this winding connect to the two elements of the dipole antenna. Often, the exact middle of this winding (the center tap) is connected to ground to establish a zero-voltage reference point.

When an RF signal arrives from the dipole antenna, the equal and opposite currents flow into the secondary winding. This alternating current generates a fluctuating magnetic field inside the ferrite core. That magnetic field induces a corresponding voltage and current into the primary winding, which then flows cleanly into the coaxial cable.

Because there is no direct physical connection for the outer shield to spill current onto the antenna—or because the magnetic core actively chokes off any current that isn't equal and opposite—common-mode currents are completely blocked. Furthermore, by changing the ratio of wire turns between the primary and secondary windings, a transformer balun can also match different impedances (such as converting a 300-ohm antenna to a 75-ohm coax).

How Marchand RF Baluns Work

At very high frequencies (microwave and millimeter-wave bands used in 5G, radar, and Wi-Fi), ferrite cores become inefficient and absorb too much signal. In these applications, engineers use the Marchand balun. Instead of magnetic coils, the Marchand balun uses the physical length of transmission lines and capacitive coupling to manipulate signal phases.

A Marchand balun is made of specifically measured sections of transmission line (either microstrip tracks on a circuit board or actual coaxial segments) that are equal to one-quarter of the target RF signal's wavelength (\(\lambda/4\)).

Here is how the Marchand balun converts the signal:

  1. The Unbalanced Input: The unbalanced coaxial line extends into the balun as a continuous conductor that is one-half wavelength (\(\lambda/2\)) long, ending in an open circuit.
  2. The Coupled Sections: Two separate quarter-wavelength (\(\lambda/4\)) conductors are placed extremely close to the main input line—one alongside the first half, and the second alongside the back half. One end of each of these smaller lines is shorted to ground, while the other ends meet at the center to form the two balanced ports for the dipole antenna.
  3. Phase Cancellation: As the RF wave travels along the main unbalanced line, it couples electromagnetically across the tiny gap into the two adjacent \(\lambda/4\) sections. Because of the physical geometry and the way the grounds and open circuits reflect the RF waves, the signal coupled into the first section is delayed by +90 degrees, while the signal in the second section is shifted by -90 degrees.

The total difference between +90 degrees and -90 degrees is 180 degrees. Consequently, the two output ports feeding the dipole receive signals of equal magnitude that are exactly 180 degrees out of phase. Because the signal is transferred entirely through quarter-wave electromagnetic coupling, the direct path for common-mode currents to travel down the outside of the coaxial shield is broken, resulting in a purely balanced antenna feed across a very wide bandwidth.
