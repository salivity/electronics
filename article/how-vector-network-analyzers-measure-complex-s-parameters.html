How Vector Network Analyzers Measure Complex S-Parameters

This article provides a comprehensive overview of how vector network analyzers (VNAs) measure complex scattering parameters (S-parameters) to evaluate electronic components. You will learn the core mechanics of signal separation, phase and magnitude detection, and how these measurements characterize signal reflection and transmission in radio frequency (RF) filters and antennas.

Understanding Complex S-Parameters

At high frequencies, standard voltage and current measurements become impractical because wavelengths are comparable to the physical dimensions of the circuits. Instead, engineers use S-parameters to quantify how RF energy propagates through a multi-port network.

S-parameters are "complex" because they measure two distinct properties of a traveling wave:

  • Magnitude: The ratio of the output wave's amplitude to the incident wave's amplitude.
  • Phase: The time delay or phase shift introduced by the Device Under Test (DUT).

In a standard two-port measurement, the VNA captures four fundamental parameters:

  • S11 (Input Reflection): Signal injected at Port 1 and reflected back to Port 1.
  • S21 (Forward Transmission): Signal injected at Port 1 and exiting at Port 2.
  • S12 (Reverse Transmission): Signal injected at Port 2 and exiting at Port 1.
  • S22 (Output Reflection): Signal injected at Port 2 and reflected back to Port 2.

The Internal Architecture of a VNA

To measure these parameters, a VNA relies on four internal hardware blocks working in sequence:

  1. RF Signal Source: The VNA generates a known, swept-frequency sinusoidal signal with a precise amplitude and phase. This serves as the incident wave injected into the DUT.
  2. Signal Separation (Directional Couplers): As the incident wave hits the DUT, part of it reflects back, and part of it passes through. The VNA uses directional couplers or bridges to isolate these waves. One coupler extracts a small sample of the outgoing incident wave (used as a reference), while another coupler captures only the wave reflected back from the DUT.
  3. Frequency Conversion and Receivers: High-frequency RF signals are difficult to process directly. The VNA uses local oscillators and mixers to down-convert the reference, reflected, and transmitted waves into a lower Intermediate Frequency (IF). This down-conversion preserves both the amplitude and phase relationships of the original RF signals.
  4. Digital Signal Processing: Analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) digitize the IF signals. The VNA's processor then compares the reflected and transmitted waves against the reference wave to calculate the complex ratios that make up the S-parameters.

The Critical Role of Calibration

A VNA cannot make accurate measurements without calibration. The coaxial cables, adapters, and internal components of the VNA introduce their own reflections, losses, and phase shifts.

Before testing a component, engineers perform a calibration—such as Short-Open-Load-Thru (SOLT)—right at the ends of the test cables. By measuring known physical standards, the VNA calculates an error model and mathematically removes the effects of the test setup. This process, known as error correction, establishes a precise "measurement plane" directly at the ports of the DUT.

Characterizing RF Filters

Filters are generally two-port devices designed to pass specific frequency bands while rejecting others. A VNA characterizes a filter by simultaneously measuring reflection (S11 and S22) and transmission (S21 and S12).

  • Insertion Loss and Passband (S21 Magnitude): By looking at the magnitude of S21 across a frequency sweep, engineers see how much signal successfully passes through the filter. In the filter’s passband, S21 should be close to 0 dB (total transmission). In the stopband, S21 drops significantly, indicating signal rejection.
  • Group Delay (S21 Phase): The complex nature of VNA measurements allows engineers to track the rate of change of the S21 phase over frequency, known as group delay. A flat group delay ensures that complex modulated signals pass through the filter without distortion.
  • Impedance Matching (S11 and S22): When a filter blocks a signal in its stopband, it usually does so by reflecting the energy back toward the source. However, in the passband, reflection must be minimal. A low S11 magnitude confirms the filter's input impedance matches the system impedance (typically 50 ohms).

Characterizing Antennas

Antennas are one-port devices that transition guided waves on a circuit board into radiating free-space waves. Because the transmitted energy leaves the system as radiation, a VNA characterizes an antenna primarily through its reflection coefficient, S11.

  • Resonant Frequency and Bandwidth: When sweeping frequencies, the VNA measures how much power reflects back from the antenna feed point. The frequency where the S11 magnitude drops to its lowest point is the antenna’s resonant frequency—the point where it radiates energy most efficiently.
  • Return Loss and VSWR: The magnitude of S11 is often converted into Return Loss (measured in dB) or Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR). These metrics tell engineers what percentage of power is successfully accepted by the antenna versus reflected back into the transmitter, which could otherwise cause hardware damage.
  • Impedance Tuning (The Smith Chart): Measuring the phase of S11 is vital for antenna matching. The VNA plots the complex S11 data (both magnitude and phase) onto a polar plot called a Smith Chart. This chart reveals whether the antenna's input impedance is inductive or capacitive at a given frequency. Engineers use this phase data to select the exact inductors and capacitors needed to build a matching network, pulling the antenna's impedance to the ideal 50-ohm center of the chart.
