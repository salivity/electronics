How Yagi-Uda Antenna Elements Focus UHF Beam Directivity

This article provides a concise overview of how parasitic director and reflector elements manipulate electromagnetic waves to create high-gain, focused beam directivity in a Yagi-Uda antenna array used for Ultra High Frequency (UHF) reception electronics. By exploring the principles of mutual impedance, phase shifting, and constructive interference, this guide explains how passive metal rods transform a standard omnidirectional dipole into a highly directional receiver capable of isolating weak UHF signals from background noise.

The Role of the Driven Element

To understand how parasitic elements focus a signal, you must first understand the driven element. In a Yagi-Uda array, the driven element is typically a half-wave dipole connected directly to the UHF receiver via a coaxial feedline. By itself, a dipole receives radio frequency (RF) energy equally well from the front and the back, creating a bidirectional pattern. To focus this reception into a single forward direction, parasitic elements are placed parallel to the driven element along a central supporting boom.

How Parasitic Elements Work

Parasitic elements—consisting of one reflector and one or more directors—are not physically connected to the reception electronics. Instead, they operate through electromagnetic coupling. When an incoming UHF radio wave strikes the antenna array, it induces small alternating currents in all the elements simultaneously.

The parasitic elements absorb this RF energy and immediately re-radiate it. Because the elements are placed at very specific distances from one another, the re-radiated waves interact with the original incoming wave. By precisely tuning the length and spacing of these parasitic rods, the antenna forces the re-radiated waves to combine with the incoming wave in a specific phase relationship.

The Reflector: Blocking Rear Signals

The reflector is placed behind the driven element, away from the signal source. It is engineered to be slightly longer than the resonant half-wave driven element (usually about 5% longer) and is spaced approximately 0.15 to 0.25 wavelengths behind it.

Because the reflector is longer than the resonant frequency of the incoming UHF wave, it exhibits inductive reactance. This inductance causes a phase lag in the current induced within the reflector. When the reflector re-radiates the UHF energy, the wave travels forward toward the driven element and arrives completely in-phase with the primary incoming signal. This causes constructive interference at the driven element, boosting the signal strength. Conversely, waves traveling backward past the reflector experience destructive interference, effectively canceling out reception from the rear of the antenna and creating a high front-to-back ratio.

The Directors: Focusing the Forward Beam

Directors are placed in front of the driven element, pointing directly toward the UHF transmitter. Unlike the reflector, directors are cut slightly shorter than the driven element (typically 5% to 10% shorter) and are spaced roughly 0.1 to 0.35 wavelengths apart.

Because the directors are shorter than the resonant wavelength, they exhibit capacitive reactance. This capacitance causes the induced current in the directors to lead the phase of the incoming wave. As a UHF wave travels across the directors toward the driven element, each director absorbs and re-radiates the energy slightly ahead of phase. This creates a localized phase velocity change, pulling the electromagnetic wavefront inward and funneling it toward the driven element.

Adding more directors along the boom narrows the antenna's beamwidth and increases forward gain. However, each subsequent director contributes slightly less gain than the one before it, due to the diminishing strength of the coupled electromagnetic field further away from the driven element.

Impact on UHF Reception Electronics

UHF signals (ranging from 300 MHz to 3 GHz) have short wavelengths, usually measured in centimeters. This makes Yagi-Uda arrays exceptionally practical for UHF, as multiple directors can be added to a relatively short boom to achieve high directivity.

For UHF reception electronics, the physical focusing provided by the reflector and directors offers three major benefits:

  • Increased Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR): By rejecting signals from the rear and sides, the array prevents off-axis interference and multipath reflections from reaching the receiver's sensitive low-noise amplifier (LNA).
  • Passive Amplification: The constructive interference at the driven element delivers a higher voltage to the receiver's front-end circuitry without requiring active power.
  • Impedance Matching: While parasitic elements drastically improve directivity, their close proximity lowers the radiation resistance of the driven element. Therefore, UHF Yagi-Uda antennas often use a folded dipole as the driven element or incorporate a matching network (balun) to ensure a stable 50-ohm or 75-ohm impedance match for the receiving electronics.
