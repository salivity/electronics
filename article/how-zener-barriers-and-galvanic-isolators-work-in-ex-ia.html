How Zener Barriers and Galvanic Isolators Work in Ex ia

This article explains how Zener safety barriers and galvanic isolators prevent explosions in Ex ia intrinsically safe environments by strictly limiting electrical energy. In hazardous locations where flammable gases or dust are present, even a microscopic spark can cause a catastrophic ignition. These sparks are often triggered by the sudden release of stored energy within the capacitance and inductance of field wiring and electronic components. By clamping voltage and restricting current, Zener barriers and galvanic isolators ensure that neither capacitive nor inductive energy can accumulate to levels capable of causing a thermal or electrical spark.

Understanding Ex ia and Stored Energy

The "Ex ia" certification is the most stringent level of intrinsic safety, requiring a circuit to remain incapable of causing an ignition even with two simultaneous faults. To achieve this, engineers must manage two specific types of energy storage in a circuit:

  • Capacitive Energy (\(E = \frac{1}{2}CV^2\)): Stored in the electric field of capacitors and long cable runs. If a wire shorts, this energy releases instantly as a high-current discharge spark. Because voltage (\(V\)) is squared in this formula, controlling voltage is the primary method for limiting capacitive energy.
  • Inductive Energy (\(E = \frac{1}{2}LI^2\)): Stored in the magnetic field of inductors, coils, and wiring. If a live wire is suddenly broken or disconnected, the collapsing magnetic field tries to maintain the current flow, creating a high-voltage arc across the break. Because current (\(I\)) is squared in this formula, restricting current is the primary method for limiting inductive energy.

Both Zener barriers and galvanic isolators are placed in the non-hazardous "safe area" to regulate the power going into the hazardous "Ex ia" area, ensuring that \(V\) and \(I\) never reach dangerous thresholds.

How Zener Safety Barriers Limit Energy

A Zener safety barrier is a passive network of three core components: a fuse, Zener diodes, and a current-limiting resistor. It relies on a high-integrity connection to the earth ground to divert excess energy away from the hazardous area.

  1. Limiting Capacitive Energy (Voltage Clamping): The Zener diodes are connected in parallel with the circuit, pointing toward the ground. Under normal operating conditions, the diodes do not conduct. However, if a fault causes the voltage to spike above the barrier’s rated safety threshold, the Zener diodes instantly break down and conduct the excess voltage directly to the ground. By strictly clamping the maximum voltage (\(V_o\)), the barrier makes it impossible for dangerous levels of capacitive energy to build up in the hazardous area. For Ex ia applications, three redundant Zener diodes are typically used so the barrier remains safe even if two diodes fail.
  2. Limiting Inductive Energy (Current Restriction): Positioned after the Zener diodes is an infallible, precision resistor wired in series with the hazardous area loop. This resistor dictates the absolute maximum current (\(I_o\)) that can ever flow into the field, even if the field wiring suffers a dead short. By physically choking the current flow, the resistor prevents inductive components and cables from storing enough magnetic energy to create an ignition-capable arc if a wire breaks.
  3. The Fuse: Located at the input of the barrier, the fuse protects the Zener diodes. When a high-voltage fault occurs and the diodes shunt the massive current to the ground, the fuse blows in milliseconds, permanently opening the circuit and stopping the flow of energy entirely.

How Galvanic Isolators Limit Energy

Galvanic isolators (or isolated barriers) are active devices that use an internal isolation component—such as a transformer, optocoupler, or capacitor—to completely separate the safe-area circuit from the hazardous-area circuit. There is no direct physical wiring path between the input and the output, eliminating the need for a dedicated safety ground.

  1. Energy Transfer Restriction: The core isolation component is physically engineered to saturate or cap out at a very low power threshold. For example, a miniature transformer inside the isolator has a magnetic core that will saturate if a fault surge hits the input. Once saturated, it physically cannot transfer any additional energy across the isolation gap to the hazardous side.
  2. Limiting Capacitive and Inductive Energy: Even though the primary protection comes from the galvanic isolation gap, the output stage of the isolator (the side connected to the hazardous area) still incorporates a secondary network of Zener diodes and current-limiting resistors. Just like in a passive Zener barrier, these output components clamp the maximum voltage (\(V_o\)) to prevent capacitive sparks and restrict the maximum current (\(I_o\)) to prevent inductive arcs.

Managing Cable Parameters

Because Zener barriers and galvanic isolators strictly cap voltage and current, every device is given specific entity parameters: Maximum Output Voltage (\(U_o\) or \(V_o\)) and Maximum Output Current (\(I_o\)).

From these values, safety standards dictate the Maximum External Capacitance (\(C_o\)) and Maximum External Inductance (\(L_o\)) that can be safely connected to the barrier. System designers calculate the capacitance and inductance of the field device plus every foot of cable running into the hazardous zone. As long as the total circuit capacitance is less than \(C_o\) and the total inductance is less than \(L_o\), the Zener barrier or galvanic isolator guarantees that any spark resulting from a short circuit or severed wire will lack the thermal energy required to ignite the surrounding atmosphere.
