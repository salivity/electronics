IPC-2152 vs Legacy Charts for PCB Trace Width Calculation

This article provides a direct comparison between the IPC-2152 standard and legacy charts like IPC-2221 for calculating printed circuit board (PCB) trace widths. It explains how IPC-2152 improves accuracy in predicting permissible temperature rise by replacing outdated 1950s data with modern empirical testing. Readers will learn how the updated standard accounts for critical thermal variables such as copper planes, board thickness, substrate materials, and operating environments to optimize modern electronic designs.

The Flaws of Legacy Charts (IPC-2221)

For decades, PCB designers relied on charts from IPC-2221 (and its predecessor, IPC-D-275) to determine the necessary trace width for a given current and permissible temperature rise. However, the data behind these legacy charts originated from a single set of experiments conducted in the 1950s.

These historical tests measured traces only on the surface of thin, air-suspended phenolic boards. To estimate internal trace performance, the creators simply halved the external current ratings. Because these charts ignored the physical realities of modern multi-layer boards, they often led to overly conservative designs with unnecessarily wide traces, or occasionally, unsafe overheating in dense layouts.

Modern Empirical Data in IPC-2152

IPC-2152, titled "Standard for Determining Current Carrying Capacity in Printed Board Design," replaced the guesswork of legacy charts with rigorous, modern empirical data and finite element analysis. Instead of relying on a single baseline chart, IPC-2152 provides a comprehensive set of charts and modifiers based on actual testing of modern PCB manufacturing materials and stack-ups.

Accurate Internal vs. External Trace Sizing

Legacy charts assumed internal traces always run hotter than external traces because they are trapped inside the board. IPC-2152 proved this assumption false. The fiberglass and resin materials (like FR-4) used in modern PCBs conduct heat much better than still air. Consequently, IPC-2152 demonstrates that internal traces often cool more efficiently than external traces, allowing designers to use much narrower internal traces than legacy charts previously permitted.

Accounting for Copper Planes

One of the most significant improvements in IPC-2152 is the inclusion of internal copper planes in thermal calculations. Copper is an exceptional thermal conductor. When a current-carrying trace is routed near a solid copper power or ground plane, the plane acts as a massive heat sink. It pulls heat away from the trace and spreads it across the entire board. IPC-2152 quantifies the distance between the trace and the plane, allowing designers to drastically reduce trace widths for a given temperature rise when copper planes are nearby.

Board Thickness and Substrate Material Variables

Legacy standards treated all circuit boards the same, regardless of their physical dimensions or materials. IPC-2152 introduces specific modifiers for these properties:

  • Board Thickness: Thicker boards have more thermal mass and dissipate heat better than thinner boards. IPC-2152 allows for smaller trace widths on thicker substrates.
  • Thermal Conductivity: Different dielectric materials transfer heat at different rates. IPC-2152 factors in the specific thermal conductivity of the chosen substrate—whether it is standard FR-4, high-frequency PTFE, or polyimide—ensuring accurate temperature rise predictions.

Environmental Considerations: Air vs. Vacuum

Legacy charts assumed all electronics operated in standard, sea-level air conditions where natural convection helps cool the board. IPC-2152 includes specific data for space and high-altitude applications where air is absent. In a vacuum, heat cannot escape via convection; it can only dissipate through conduction into the board and thermal radiation. IPC-2152 gives aerospace engineers the exact data needed to size traces safely when convection cooling does not exist.

Parallel Trace Coupling

While legacy charts treated every trace as an isolated element, IPC-2152 addresses the reality of dense routing. When multiple current-carrying traces are routed closely together in parallel, their heat fields combine. IPC-2152 provides guidelines to calculate this mutual heating effect, preventing unexpected temperature spikes in tightly packed circuit board layouts.
