Microstrip vs Stripline Propagation Velocity in High-Speed PCBs

This article explores the differences in signal propagation velocity between outer-layer microstrip transmission lines and inner-layer buried stripline traces in high-frequency printed circuit boards (PCBs). By examining the surrounding dielectric environments of both trace geometries, we will explain why signals travel significantly faster on the surface of a PCB than they do when buried within its internal layers, and how these differences impact high-speed circuit design and timing.

The Role of the Dielectric Constant

In high-frequency PCB electronics, the speed at which an electrical signal travels along a trace—its propagation velocity—is dictated by the material surrounding the conductor. Electromagnetic waves travel fastest in a vacuum (the speed of light, \(c\)). When a signal travels through an insulating material, or dielectric, its speed is reduced.

The degree to which a material slows down a signal is measured by its relative dielectric constant (\(D_k\) or \(\varepsilon_r\)). Air has a \(D_k\) of approximately 1.0, offering almost no resistance to signal speed. Standard PCB substrate materials, such as FR-4, have a much higher \(D_k\), typically ranging from 3.8 to 4.8. The fundamental rule of signal propagation is simple: the lower the dielectric constant of the surrounding medium, the faster the signal travels.

Stripline Propagation Velocity

A stripline is a transmission line trace routed on an internal layer of a PCB. It is completely buried within the board's insulating substrate and sandwiched between two reference ground planes—one above and one below.

Because a stripline is entirely encased in a homogeneous dielectric material (the PCB substrate and prepreg), 100% of its electromagnetic field lines travel through this solid material. Therefore, the propagation velocity of a stripline is determined strictly by the \(D_k\) of the PCB substrate:

\[V_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\]

In standard FR-4 with a \(D_k\) of 4.0, the signal travels at half the speed of light, or roughly 6 inches per nanosecond (approximately 165 to 170 picoseconds per inch of delay).

Microstrip Propagation Velocity

A microstrip is a transmission line routed on the outermost, exposed layers of a PCB (top or bottom), referenced to a single internal ground plane directly beneath it. Unlike a stripline, a microstrip is exposed to two different mediums: the solid PCB substrate below the trace, and the air (along with a thin layer of solder mask) above the trace.

As a high-frequency signal travels down a microstrip, its electromagnetic field lines split. While the majority of the field is concentrated in the PCB substrate between the trace and the ground plane, a significant portion fringes upward into the air. Because the field travels through a mix of substrate (\(D_k \approx 4.0\)) and air (\(D_k \approx 1.0\)), engineers must use an "effective dielectric constant" (\(\varepsilon_{eff}\)) to calculate signal speed.

Because air lowers the overall average dielectric constant, \(\varepsilon_{eff}\) is always lower than the substrate's actual \(\varepsilon_r\). The propagation velocity for a microstrip is calculated as:

\[V_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}\]

For a microstrip on the same FR-4 material, the \(\varepsilon_{eff}\) might drop to around 2.8 to 3.2. Consequently, the signal travels much faster—roughly 7 inches per nanosecond (approximately 140 to 150 picoseconds per inch of delay).

Key Impacts on High-Frequency PCB Design

Understanding the velocity difference between microstrip and stripline configurations is critical for high-speed digital and RF PCB layout for several reasons:

  • Length Matching and Timing: When routing parallel data buses (such as DDR memory) where signals must arrive at the receiver simultaneously, engineers cannot simply match the physical lengths of the traces if they are routed on different layers. A trace routed as a microstrip will deliver its signal faster than a stripline of the exact same physical length. Designers must match "electrical length" (time delay) rather than physical length.
  • Velocity Dispersion: Because a microstrip uses a mixed dielectric medium (air and fiberglass/epoxy), different frequency components of a signal can travel at slightly different speeds. This causes phase distortion and dispersion at extremely high frequencies. Striplines, being surrounded by a uniform material, do not suffer from this issue and offer better signal integrity for ultra-high-speed serial links.
  • Component Density: Because signals travel slower in striplines, the physical wavelength of a given frequency is shorter. While this requires careful delay management in digital circuits, it can actually be an advantage in RF design, allowing engineers to create physically smaller quarter-wave transformers or couplers on internal layers compared to the surface.
