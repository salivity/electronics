Overdamped, Critically Damped, and Underdamped RLC Responses

This article provides a concise breakdown of the three transient responses found in second-order RLC electronic circuits: overdamped, critically damped, and underdamped. By examining the relationship between resistance, inductance, and capacitance, it explains how damping ratios and characteristic roots determine whether a circuit returns to equilibrium slowly, as fast as possible without overshoot, or through decaying oscillations.

Understanding the Second-Order RLC Circuit

A second-order RLC circuit contains a resistor (R), an inductor (L), and a capacitor (C). When a sudden change occurs—such as a switch opening or closing—the energy stored in the magnetic field of the inductor and the electric field of the capacitor is released.

The behavior of the voltage and current during this transition is called the transient response. This response is governed by a second-order differential equation characterized by two primary parameters:

  • Neper Frequency (\(\alpha\)): Represents the rate at which the response is damped (attenuated) by the resistor. For a series RLC circuit, \(\alpha = \frac{R}{2L}\). For a parallel RLC circuit, \(\alpha = \frac{1}{2RC}\).
  • Resonant Frequency (\(\omega_0\)): Represents the natural frequency at which the LC tank would oscillate without resistance. For both series and parallel circuits, \(\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\).

The ratio of these two parameters defines the damping ratio (\(\zeta\)): \[\zeta = \frac{\alpha}{\omega_0}\]

The relationship between \(\alpha\) and \(\omega_0\) (or the value of \(\zeta\)) dictates the roots of the circuit's characteristic equation (\(s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}\)) and separates the transient response into three distinct categories.

1. Overdamped Response (\(\alpha > \omega_0\) or \(\zeta > 1\))

An overdamped response occurs when the resistance in the circuit is high enough to completely suppress any natural oscillations between the inductor and the capacitor.

  • Mathematical Roots: The characteristic equation yields two distinct, real, and negative roots (\(s_1 \neq s_2\)).
  • Physical Behavior: The high resistance dissipates energy rapidly at first, which restricts the flow of current (in series) or drops the voltage (in parallel). Because the energy transfer is heavily restricted, it takes a relatively long time for the capacitor and inductor to fully discharge or reach their final steady-state values.
  • Waveform: The signal approaches its final equilibrium value slowly and smoothly with no overshoot or oscillation.

2. Critically Damped Response (\(\alpha = \omega_0\) or \(\zeta = 1\))

A critically damped response represents the exact mathematical boundary between an overdamped and an underdamped system.

  • Mathematical Roots: The characteristic equation yields two identical, real, and negative roots (\(s_1 = s_2 = -\alpha\)).
  • Physical Behavior: The resistance is tuned to the exact value needed to absorb the reactive energy in the shortest time possible without allowing the energy to bounce back and forth between the capacitor and the inductor.
  • Waveform: The signal reaches its final steady-state value in the fastest possible time without overshooting the target value or oscillating. In practical electronics, true critical damping is difficult to maintain due to component tolerances and temperature variations, so engineers often aim slightly to one side of this boundary depending on the application.

3. Underdamped Response (\(\alpha < \omega_0\) or \(\zeta < 1\))

An underdamped response occurs when the resistance is too low to immediately dissipate the energy stored in the circuit's reactive components.

  • Mathematical Roots: The characteristic equation yields two complex conjugate roots with negative real parts (\(s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm j\omega_d\), where \(\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2}\) is the damped natural frequency).
  • Physical Behavior: Energy transfers rapidly back and forth between the inductor's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field. Each time the energy transfers, the resistor dissipates a small portion of it as heat, causing the amplitude of the energy spikes to decay exponentially over time.
  • Waveform: The signal shoots past its final steady-state value (overshoot), reverses direction, and oscillates above and below the target value. This behavior is commonly referred to as "ringing." The oscillations gradually shrink until the signal settles at equilibrium.
