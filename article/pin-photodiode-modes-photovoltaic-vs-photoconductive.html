PIN Photodiode Modes: Photovoltaic vs Photoconductive

PIN photodiodes operate in two distinct modes depending on the needs of the electronic circuit: photovoltaic mode and photoconductive mode. Photovoltaic mode uses zero bias to eliminate dark current, making it the preferred choice for low-noise, high-precision light measurements at lower frequencies. Conversely, photoconductive mode applies a reverse bias voltage across the diode, which widens the depletion region and reduces junction capacitance, allowing the photodiode to achieve the fast response times required for high-speed electronics and optical communications.

Understanding the PIN Photodiode Structure

A PIN photodiode consists of three semiconductor regions: a heavily doped P-type layer, an undoped Intrinsic (I) layer, and a heavily doped N-type layer. When photons with sufficient energy strike the intrinsic layer, they create electron-hole pairs. An electric field within this region sweeps the carriers to their respective terminals, generating a photocurrent. How an external circuit biases these terminals dictates the behavior, speed, and noise floor of the photodiode.

Zero-Bias Photovoltaic Mode for Low-Noise Precision

In photovoltaic mode, the PIN photodiode is operated with zero external bias voltage across its terminals. Instead, the generated photocurrent is typically fed directly into a transimpedance amplifier that holds the voltage across the diode at virtually zero volts.

Engineers choose photovoltaic mode for precision light measurements for the following reasons:

  • Elimination of Dark Current: Dark current is the small electric current that flows through a photosensitive device even when no photons are entering it. Because there is no applied voltage in photovoltaic mode, dark current is virtually eliminated.
  • Reduction of Shot Noise: Shot noise is directly proportional to the amount of dark current present. By removing the dark current, shot noise drops to near zero, leaving Johnson (thermal) noise of the shunt resistance as the only major noise source.
  • Maximized Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR): With the noise floor minimized, the photodiode can accurately detect extremely weak light signals.
  • High Linearity: Zero-bias operation provides exceptional linearity across a wide range of light levels, ensuring accurate, predictable measurements.

The main trade-off of photovoltaic mode is speed. Without an external bias voltage, the intrinsic depletion region remains relatively narrow, resulting in higher junction capacitance and slower charge collection times. This limits photovoltaic mode to low-frequency applications such as medical instrumentation, light meters, and precision spectroscopy.

Reverse-Biased Photoconductive Mode for High-Speed Electronics

In photoconductive mode, an external reverse bias voltage is applied to the PIN photodiode—meaning the positive terminal of the voltage source is connected to the cathode (N-layer) and the negative terminal is connected to the anode (P-layer).

Engineers choose photoconductive mode for high-speed electronics for the following reasons:

  • Wider Depletion Region: The reverse bias voltage pulls free charge carriers away from the intrinsic region, widening the depletion zone to encompass almost the entire intrinsic layer.
  • Reduced Junction Capacitance: A wider depletion region acts like a capacitor with plates that are further apart. This significantly reduces the photodiode's junction capacitance. Because the response time of the circuit is limited by the RC time constant (Resistance × Capacitance), lowering the capacitance directly increases the operational speed and bandwidth.
  • Increased Carrier Drift Velocity: The applied reverse voltage creates a strong internal electric field. When photons generate electron-hole pairs, this strong field accelerates the carriers toward the terminals much faster than the natural diffusion process used in zero-bias mode. This drastically reduces the rise and fall times of the output signal.

The trade-off for this high speed is increased noise. The applied reverse voltage forces a small reverse saturation current—dark current—through the diode even in total darkness. This dark current introduces shot noise, which raises the noise floor and makes the diode less sensitive to very faint light. However, in applications like fiber-optic communications, LiDAR, and high-speed pulse detection, the need for gigahertz-level switching speeds far outweighs the presence of minor dark current noise.
