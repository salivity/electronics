Replay Sensor Glitches with Deep-Memory AWGs to Test Receivers

Engineers use deep-memory arbitrary waveform generators (AWGs) to stress-test receiver electronics by capturing real-world sensor anomalies, storing these massive datasets in onboard memory, and reconstructing the exact analog signals in a laboratory environment. This article explains the step-by-step process of how high-resolution digitizers capture unpredictable physical glitches, why deep memory is required to preserve high sample rates over long durations, and how engineers manipulate these recorded waveforms to push receiver hardware to its absolute operational limits.

1. Capturing the Real-World Glitch

Real-world sensors—such as automotive radar, optical transceivers, and industrial transducers—occasionally produce anomalous outputs caused by electromagnetic interference, power fluctuations, or environmental extremes. Because these glitches are erratic, standard mathematical waveform modeling cannot replicate them accurately.

To bring these anomalies into the lab, engineers first use a high-bandwidth digital storage oscilloscope (DSO) or digitizer in the field. The oscilloscope records the sensor’s raw analog output at a high sampling rate, converting the physical voltage variations into a massive array of digital time-series data points.

2. The Role of Deep Memory

To accurately reproduce a fast, sub-nanosecond glitch embedded within several seconds of normal sensor operation, an AWG requires both a high sampling rate and a long playback duration. This is where deep memory becomes critical.

The relationship between playback time, sample rate, and memory depth is defined by a simple formula: Playback Time = Memory Depth / Sample Rate

Standard AWGs with shallow memory force engineers into a compromise: they must either lower the sample rate (which smooths over and erases the high-frequency glitch) or shorten the playback time (which removes the operational context surrounding the glitch). Deep-memory AWGs—often boasting gigasamples of waveform memory—eliminate this trade-off. They allow the instrument to stream minutes of continuous data while maintaining the multi-gigasample-per-second rates required to resolve sudden, razor-sharp voltage spikes.

3. Data Transfer and Waveform Conditioning

Once the field data is captured, the digital file (often in CSV, binary, or MATLAB format) is imported into the AWG’s waveform creation software. Before outputting the signal, engineers condition the data to isolate or exaggerate the failure mechanism:

  • Normalization and Scaling: Adjusting the digital values to take full advantage of the AWG’s vertical resolution (typically 12-bit to 16-bit) without clipping.
  • Filtering: Removing the oscilloscope's inherent noise floor from the capture so the AWG only outputs the sensor's behavior.
  • Margin Modification: Mathematically stretching the glitch in time, increasing its amplitude, or overlaying additional Gaussian noise to turn a borderline real-world glitch into a worst-case scenario.

4. High-Speed DAC Reconstruction

When the file is loaded into the AWG's deep memory, the instrument acts as a high-precision playback system. A high-stability internal clock reads the stored digital samples sequentially and feeds them into a fast Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC).

The DAC translates the binary numbers back into discrete voltage levels, and an analog reconstruction filter smooths the steps into a continuous, real-world waveform. The resulting output at the AWG's coaxial port is an exact electrical twin of the failing sensor from the field.

5. Stress-Testing with Advanced Sequencing

Simply playing a glitch once is rarely enough to validate receiver electronics. Deep-memory AWGs utilize hardware sequencing to turn a single captured anomaly into a rigorous stress test.

Instead of filling the memory with repetitive normal data, engineers break the memory into segments. One long segment contains normal sensor behavior, while smaller segments contain different captured glitches. Using the AWG’s sequencer, the engineer can loop the normal signal infinitely and dynamically trigger the glitch segments at randomized intervals, varying amplitudes, or rapidly increasing frequencies.

By injecting these sequenced signals directly into the receiver's front-end, engineers can observe how the device under test (DUT) recovers. They can pinpoint the exact thresholds where the receiver's clock data recovery (CDR) loses lock, where analog-to-digital converters saturate, or where error-correction algorithms fail, ensuring the hardware is robust before mass production.
