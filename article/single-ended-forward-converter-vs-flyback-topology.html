Single-Ended Forward Converter vs Flyback Topology

Power supply designers typically transition from a flyback topology to a single-ended forward converter when designing medium-power electronics ranging from 100W to 250W, particularly for applications requiring high output currents and low voltage ripple. This article explores the specific engineering criteria behind this decision, examining how the fundamental differences in transformer operation, component stress, efficiency, and filtering requirements make the forward converter the superior choice for demanding medium-power applications.

Fundamental Operational Differences

The primary reason to choose a forward converter over a flyback topology lies in how the magnetic components handle energy.

A flyback converter does not use a true transformer; it uses a coupled inductor. When the primary switch is closed, energy is stored in the magnetic core's air gap. When the switch opens, that stored energy is released to the secondary side and the output load. This store-and-release cycle limits the amount of power the core can efficiently transfer before becoming prohibitively large and expensive.

Conversely, a single-ended forward converter uses a true transformer to transfer energy directly from the input to the output while the primary switch is conducting. Because the transformer does not need to store energy, the core can be smaller, and it operates with significantly lower flux excursions. When the primary switch opens, an output inductor on the secondary side freewheels to maintain continuous current to the load, while a reset winding returns any magnetizing energy back to the input source.

Power Levels and Current Requirements

While the flyback topology is the undisputed king of low-power designs (under 75W to 100W) due to its low component count, its advantages rapidly diminish as power demands rise. Designers select the single-ended forward converter when parameters cross the following thresholds:

  • Power Output Above 100W: Between 100W and 250W, the physical size of a flyback transformer becomes impractical due to the large air gap required to prevent core saturation. The forward converter handles this power range with a much smaller magnetic footprint.
  • High Output Current (Above 10A): Flyback converters deliver energy in high-current pulses during the switch-off time. At high output currents, the Root Mean Square (RMS) current in the secondary winding and output capacitors becomes excessively high, leading to severe resistive (\(I^2R\)) losses. A forward converter delivers continuous current via its output inductor, drastically reducing secondary RMS currents and improving thermal performance.
  • Low Output Voltage Designs: When designing a 150W power supply with a 5V or 12V output, the resulting high current heavily favors the forward topology. A flyback converter might still be used at 150W only if the output voltage is very high (e.g., 100V+), which keeps the output current low.

Output Ripple and Filtering

In medium-power electronics, maintaining a clean, stable DC output is often critical. Because the flyback topology operates with discontinuous currents on the secondary side, it inherently produces high output voltage ripple and high peak-to-average current ratios. Mitigating this requires massive banks of low-ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance) output capacitors, which consume valuable board space and decrease the overall lifespan of the power supply.

The single-ended forward converter includes an output filter inductor alongside the output capacitor. This LC filter smooths the current before it reaches the capacitor and the load. As a result, the output ripple is naturally much lower, the stress on the output capacitors is vastly reduced, and the physical size of the capacitive filtering stage is minimized.

Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) and Efficiency

High peak currents in a flyback converter generate significant Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) and high-frequency noise. As power scales into the medium range, filtering out this noise to pass regulatory compliance becomes a major design hurdle. Furthermore, the leakage inductance in a flyback transformer causes high voltage spikes on the primary MOSFET, requiring dissipative snubber circuits that directly reduce overall system efficiency.

The forward converter operates with much lower peak currents for the same power throughput. This results in lower radiated and conducted EMI, simplifying the input filter design. Additionally, because the forward converter uses a dedicated reset winding (or an active clamp circuit) to reset the transformer core, the energy that would normally cause destructive voltage spikes is safely recycled back to the input capacitor, boosting overall power conversion efficiency to levels a medium-power flyback cannot match.

Summary of Selection Criteria

Power supply designers will ultimately accept the higher part count and increased complexity of a single-ended forward converter—specifically the addition of a secondary output inductor, a freewheeling diode, and a transformer reset mechanism—when the application demands:

  1. Power outputs between 100W and 250W.
  2. Output currents exceeding 10A to 15A.
  3. Strict limits on output voltage ripple and noise.
  4. Higher efficiency and better thermal management at medium power.
  5. Smaller magnetic and capacitive footprints than a comparable flyback design would allow.
