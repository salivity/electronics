Stacked Microvia Reliability Risks Under Thermal Cycling

This article examines the critical reliability risks that arise when copper-filled stacked microvias in multilayer electronics are subjected to repeated thermal expansion cycles. As high-density interconnect (HDI) printed circuit boards (PCBs) become increasingly compact, stacked microvias are frequently used to route signals vertically across multiple layers. However, cyclical temperature fluctuations expose these structures to severe mechanical stress due to material property mismatches. Below, we explore the primary failure mechanisms—including interfacial delamination, copper fatigue, and dielectric cracking—and how they lead to intermittent or catastrophic circuit failure.

Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE) Mismatch

The root cause of almost all thermal cycling failures in stacked microvias is the mismatch in the Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE) between the copper and the surrounding dielectric material (resin/fiberglass). Along the vertical Z-axis, the dielectric material expands and contracts at a rate significantly higher than that of the electroplated copper—often three to four times higher, especially when temperatures exceed the dielectric's glass transition temperature (Tg).

When stacked microvias are filled with solid copper, they form a rigid vertical pillar spanning multiple layers. As the PCB heats up, the surrounding laminate expands upward and downward much faster than the rigid copper column, placing immense tensile stress on the microvia stack. When the board cools, the laminate contracts, subjecting the stack to compressive stress.

Interfacial Pullout and Separation

The weakest point in a stacked microvia structure is the interface between the bottom of the upper microvia and the top target pad (capture pad) of the lower microvia. Because stacked microvias place multiple copper-to-copper interfaces in a single vertical line, the mechanical stress concentrates heavily at these junctions.

Repeated thermal expansion cycles pull these interfaces apart, leading to interfacial separation or "via pullout." This issue is frequently exacerbated by imperfect manufacturing processes. If minuscule amounts of organic residue from laser drilling remain on the target pad (due to inadequate desmear processes), the electroless copper seed layer cannot form a strong metallurgical bond with the underlying pad. Under thermal stress, this weak boundary cleanly separates, resulting in an open circuit.

Barrel Fatigue and Corner Cracking

Continuous alternating between tensile and compressive stress during thermal cycling causes metal fatigue within the copper itself. Unlike staggered microvias, which distribute vertical stress across separated XY coordinates, stacked microvias concentrate the Z-axis strain directly through a single vertical column.

Over time, the ductility of the electroplated copper is exhausted. Micro-voids within the copper fill begin to coalesce, leading to fatigue cracks. These cracks typically initiate at the corners of the microvia or along the walls of the microvia barrel, eventually propagating horizontally through the entire copper fill and severing the electrical connection.

Dielectric Cracking and Pad Cratering

When a stacked microvia is filled with copper, its high rigidity prevents it from absorbing the mechanical energy generated during thermal cycling. If the copper-to-copper bonds within the stack are strong enough to resist separation, the stress is transferred directly into the surrounding dielectric material and the copper pads anchoring the stack.

This transferred stress can exceed the fracture toughness of the resin system, causing radial cracks in the dielectric layer—a phenomenon similar to pad cratering. Once the dielectric cracks, it creates a pathway for moisture and contaminants to enter, which can lead to conductive anodic filament (CAF) growth and short-circuiting between adjacent conductors.

Intermittent Electrical Failures

One of the most dangerous risks associated with thermally stressed stacked microvias is the occurrence of intermittent failures. During the heating phase of a thermal cycle, the expanding dielectric pulls a cracked or delaminated microvia apart, breaking the electrical connection. However, as the electronic device cools down, the dielectric contracts and presses the separated copper surfaces back together, restoring electrical continuity.

Because the circuit appears functional at room temperature during standard diagnostic testing, these thermally induced intermittent opens are notoriously difficult to detect and troubleshoot, posing severe risks for mission-critical aerospace, automotive, and medical electronics.
