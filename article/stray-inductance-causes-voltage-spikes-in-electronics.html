Stray Inductance Causes Voltage Spikes in Electronics

This article provides a quick overview of how stray inductance in electronic component leads generates dangerous voltage spikes during rapid current transitions. By examining the physics of parasitic inductance, the mathematical relationship between current change and voltage, and practical mitigation techniques, you will learn why fast-switching circuits are particularly vulnerable to this phenomenon and how to protect your components.

What is Stray Inductance?

Every conductive path in an electronic circuit possesses a small, unintended amount of inductance known as stray or parasitic inductance. Component leads, printed circuit board (PCB) traces, and wire bonds all act as tiny inductors. When current flows through these conductors, a magnetic field forms around them. At low frequencies or in direct current (DC) applications, this stray inductance is negligible. However, in modern high-speed electronics, even a few nanohenries of inductance in a component lead can severely impact circuit performance.

The Physics of Fast Current Transitions

The root cause of induced voltage spikes lies in Faraday’s Law of Induction, which is expressed by the inductor voltage equation:

V = L × (di/dt)

In this formula:

  • V is the induced voltage.
  • L is the inductance (in this case, the stray inductance of the lead).
  • di/dt is the rate of change of current over time.

Inductors naturally resist changes in current. When a circuit switches on or off rapidly, the current transitions from one state to another in mere nanoseconds. This creates an extremely high di/dt value. Because the induced voltage is directly proportional to this rate of change, multiplying even a minuscule stray inductance (L) by a massive rate of current change (di/dt) results in a substantial voltage spike.

When current is abruptly cut off, the magnetic field around the component lead collapses rapidly. To keep the current flowing, the collapsing field generates a high-voltage spike of opposite polarity, often referred to as inductive kickback or voltage overshoot.

Real-World Examples in Electronics

Voltage spikes from stray inductance are most prevalent in circuits that switch substantial currents at high speeds. Common examples include:

  • Switching Power Supplies (SMPS): Transistors like MOSFETs and IGBTs switch on and off at high frequencies. Stray inductance in the transistor leads causes voltage overshoot during turn-off, which can exceed the breakdown voltage of the semiconductor and destroy it.
  • Digital Logic Gates: Fast transitions between high and low states in microprocessors and FPGAs draw rapid bursts of current. Stray inductance in the ground leads causes "ground bounce," where the component's internal ground reference temporarily spikes above the system ground, leading to false logic triggering.
  • Decoupling Capacitors: If a decoupling capacitor has long leads, the stray inductance increases its Equivalent Series Inductance (ESL). During a fast current transient, this inductance blocks the capacitor from supplying immediate charge to the integrated circuit, rendering the capacitor ineffective.

How to Minimize Stray Inductance and Voltage Spikes

To prevent destructive voltage spikes, engineers must either reduce the stray inductance (L) or slow down the current transition (di/dt). This is achieved through several design practices:

  1. Use Surface-Mount Devices (SMD): Through-hole components have long metal leads that act as significant inductors. Switching to SMD components drastically reduces lead length and stray inductance.
  2. Optimize PCB Layout: Keep high-current switching loops as short and wide as possible. Placing decoupling capacitors directly adjacent to component power pins minimizes trace inductance.
  3. Add Snubber Circuits: Implementing resistor-capacitor (RC) snubber networks or flyback diodes across switching components provides a safe path for trapped inductive energy to dissipate, clamping the voltage spike.
  4. Control Switching Speed: Increasing the gate resistor value on a MOSFET slightly slows down its turn-on and turn-off times. While this slightly decreases power efficiency, it reduces di/dt and keeps voltage spikes within safe limits.
