Symmetric Stripline vs Microstrip for RF Signal Isolation

Symmetric buried stripline routing is preferred over microstrip for isolating sensitive radio frequency (RF) signals because it completely encloses the signal trace between two solid reference ground planes within a Homogeneous dielectric material. This physical structure acts as an integrated Faraday cage, containing electromagnetic fields, drastically reducing crosstalk, and blocking external electromagnetic interference (EMI). While microstrip routing places traces on the outer layers of a printed circuit board (PCB) leaving them exposed to air and external radiation, symmetric stripline provides a uniform, shielded environment essential for maintaining high-frequency signal integrity in complex multilayer electronics.

Superior Shielding Against External EMI

The primary reason engineers choose symmetric buried stripline for sensitive RF signals is its physical isolation.

  • Microstrip Vulnerability: A microstrip trace sits on the top or bottom surface of a PCB, referenced to a single ground plane below it. Because the top of the trace is exposed to air (and solder mask), it acts like a miniature antenna. It easily radiates its own energy outward and absorbs ambient electromagnetic noise from neighboring components or external sources.
  • Stripline Containment: A symmetric buried stripline is sandwiched directly in the middle of the PCB stackup, positioned at an equal distance between a top and bottom ground plane. These two planes capture the electromagnetic fields generated by the RF signal, preventing them from escaping. Conversely, incoming radiation hits the outer ground planes and is safely shunted away before it can couple onto the inner RF trace.

True TEM Wave Propagation

Signal integrity at radio frequencies depends heavily on how the electromagnetic wave travels down the transmission line.

In a symmetric stripline, the signal trace is completely surrounded by a single type of dielectric material (the PCB substrate/prepreg). Because the environment is uniform, the signal travels in a true Transverse Electromagnetic (TEM) mode. All parts of the electromagnetic wave travel at the exact same phase velocity, which minimizes signal dispersion and phase distortion at high frequencies.

In contrast, a microstrip trace has the PCB dielectric beneath it and air above it. Because signals travel faster in air than in PCB substrate, the wave propagates in a "quasi-TEM" mode. This velocity mismatch causes phase distortion, reduces signal quality at millimeter-wave frequencies, and increases radiative losses.

Drastic Reduction in Crosstalk

In high-density multilayer electronics, traces must often be routed close together.

With microstrip routing, electric and magnetic fields fringe widely through the air above the board, easily coupling into adjacent traces and causing high levels of forward and backward crosstalk.

With symmetric buried stripline, the equal proximity to both the upper and lower ground planes tightly binds the electric and magnetic fields to the reference planes rather than allowing them to spread horizontally. Furthermore, because the propagation velocity is uniform (true TEM), far-end crosstalk (FEXT) is theoretically eliminated in a perfectly homogeneous stripline environment, allowing engineers to pack RF traces closer together without sacrificing isolation.

Why Symmetry Matters

Striplines can be designed as either asymmetric (closer to one ground plane than the other) or symmetric (equidistant to both). Symmetric routing is specifically preferred for the most sensitive RF paths for two reasons:

  1. Balanced Return Currents: Having the trace centered between the two planes ensures that the return current divides equally between the top and bottom reference planes. This balance maximizes the shielding efficiency and prevents unwanted slotline modes from developing.
  2. Predictable Impedance: Manufacturing tolerances are easier to manage when the dielectric thickness above and below the trace is identical. This ensures consistent characteristic impedance (typically 50 Ohms for RF) across the entire length of the trace, preventing signal reflections.

The Trade-offs to Consider

While symmetric buried stripline is vastly superior for RF isolation, it requires careful engineering to implement. Because the trace is buried, it must transition to the surface layers using vias to connect to components. At RF frequencies, vias introduce parasitic capacitance and inductance, and unused via barrels (stubs) act as resonant antennas that degrade the signal. Therefore, using symmetric stripline effectively requires advanced fabrication techniques like back-drilling or the use of blind and buried vias, as well as stitching vias around the trace perimeter to tie the top and bottom ground planes together.
