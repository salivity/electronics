What Creates the Built-In Potential Barrier in a PN Junction?

The built-in potential barrier across an unbiased PN junction is created by the natural diffusion of majority charge carriers across the junction, which leaves behind a region of exposed, immobile ions. As free electrons from the N-type semiconductor and holes from the P-type semiconductor diffuse and recombine, they form a charge-depleted zone known as the depletion region. The exposed positive and negative ions within this region establish an internal electric field that opposes further diffusion. This article explains the step-by-step physics of carrier concentration gradients, diffusion, ion exposure, and the resulting equilibrium that defines the built-in potential barrier in solid-state electronics.

Carrier Concentration Gradients and Diffusion

A PN junction is formed by joining a P-type semiconductor, which has a high concentration of positively charged holes, with an N-type semiconductor, which has a high concentration of negatively charged free electrons. Because of this sharp difference in carrier concentrations on either side of the metallurgical junction, a massive concentration gradient exists the moment the junction is formed.

In physics, particles naturally move from areas of high concentration to areas of low concentration—a process called diffusion. Consequently, free electrons from the N-side immediately begin to diffuse across the junction into the P-side. Simultaneously, holes from the P-side diffuse across the junction into the N-side.

Recombination and the Depletion Region

Before diffusion occurs, both the P-type and N-type materials are electrically neutral. The negatively charged mobile electrons in the N-type material are balanced by positively charged donor atoms fixed in the crystal lattice. Similarly, the positively charged holes in the P-type material are balanced by negatively charged, fixed acceptor atoms.

When the diffusing electrons cross into the P-side, they encounter a vast number of holes and quickly recombine with them, effectively neutralizing both mobile carriers. Likewise, diffusing holes recombine with free electrons on the N-side.

As these mobile carriers disappear through recombination near the interface, they leave behind the fixed donor and acceptor atoms, which are now uncovered and no longer electrically neutralized.

  • On the N-side of the junction, the loss of free electrons leaves behind a layer of positively charged donor ions.
  • On the P-side of the junction, the loss of holes leaves behind a layer of negatively charged acceptor ions.

Because this area is stripped of mobile charge carriers, it is called the depletion region (or space-charge region).

The Internal Electric Field and Drift

The uncovered positive ions on the N-side and negative ions on the P-side create a separation of charge directly across the junction. This separation of fixed charges establishes an internal electric field pointing from the positively charged N-side to the negatively charged P-side.

This electric field exerts an electrostatic force on the remaining mobile charge carriers, creating a secondary current known as drift. The electric field pushes electrons back toward the N-side and pushes holes back toward the P-side. Therefore, the internal electric field directly opposes the diffusion process that created it.

Thermal Equilibrium and the Potential Barrier

As more carriers diffuse, the depletion region widens, and the internal electric field grows stronger. Eventually, the force of the electric field becomes strong enough to completely halt the net diffusion of majority carriers across the junction.

At this point, the junction reaches thermal equilibrium. The diffusion current (majority carriers moving down the concentration gradient) is perfectly balanced by the drift current (minority carriers swept across by the electric field), resulting in a net current of zero.

In electrostatics, the presence of an electric field over a distance creates a voltage, or electric potential difference. The voltage developed across the depletion region at thermal equilibrium is the built-in potential barrier (often denoted as \(V_{bi}\) or \(V_0\)). It is called a "barrier" because majority carriers must gain enough external energy to overcome this internal voltage—typically around 0.7 volts for silicon and 0.3 volts for germanium at room temperature—before significant current can flow across the junction.

The Fermi Level Perspective

From an energy band perspective, the built-in potential barrier is the result of Fermi level alignment. Isolated P-type and N-type semiconductors have different Fermi energy levels; the Fermi level is close to the conduction band in N-type material and close to the valence band in P-type material.

In a unified system at thermal equilibrium, the Fermi level must be constant throughout the entire material. When the PN junction is formed, electrons transfer from the N-side to the P-side until the Fermi levels align. This alignment forces the conduction and valence bands to bend across the depletion region. The total amount of energy band bending corresponds directly to the magnitude of the built-in potential barrier.
