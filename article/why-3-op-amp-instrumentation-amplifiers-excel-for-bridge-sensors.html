Why 3-Op-Amp Instrumentation Amplifiers Excel for Bridge Sensors

Three-op-amp instrumentation amplifiers are the industry standard for conditioning microvolt bridge sensor signals because they simultaneously deliver ultra-high input impedance, exceptional common-mode rejection, and simple, accurate gain control. When extracting tiny differential voltages from Wheatstone bridge sensors—such as strain gauges, load cells, and pressure transducers—standard operational amplifiers struggle with circuit loading and ambient noise. This article explores the specific architectural advantages of the three-op-amp topology, explaining how its dual-stage design isolates the sensor, eliminates large common-mode voltages, and cleanly amplifies microvolt signals for accurate data acquisition.

Understanding the Microvolt Bridge Sensor Challenge

Bridge sensors typically operate in a Wheatstone bridge configuration excited by a stable DC voltage. When the physical phenomenon being measured (such as weight or pressure) changes, the bridge becomes unbalanced, producing a differential output signal.

This signal presents three major challenges for measurement electronics:

  • Microvolt Amplitude: The differential signal is extremely small, often in the microvolt (\(\mu\text{V}\)) to millivolt (\(\text{mV}\)) range, requiring high amplification.
  • High Common-Mode Voltage: The tiny differential signal rides on top of a large common-mode DC voltage, which is normally half of the excitation voltage.
  • High Output Impedance: Bridge sensors have relatively high and sensitive source impedances. Drawing current from the bridge alters the voltage reading, introducing severe measurement errors.

A simple single-op-amp differential amplifier fails here because its input impedance is relatively low and unequal between the two inputs, which loads down the sensor and destroys signal integrity. The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier (in-amp) solves these issues by splitting the amplification process into two distinct stages.

Extremely High and Balanced Input Impedance

The first stage of a three-op-amp in-amp consists of two non-inverting buffer amplifiers—one dedicated to each output leg of the bridge sensor.

Because the sensor signals feed directly into the non-inverting inputs of these two operational amplifiers, the input impedance is exceptionally high, often ranging from gigohms (\(\text{G}\Omega\)) to teraohms (\(\text{T}\Omega\)). Consequently, the amplifier draws virtually zero current from the Wheatstone bridge. This eliminates the "loading effect," ensuring the microvolt signal produced by the sensor is the exact voltage seen by the amplifier. Furthermore, because the two input op-amps are matched inside a single integrated circuit package, the impedance is balanced, preventing common-mode noise from converting into differential noise.

Superior Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

In industrial and medical measurement environments, the microvolt signal from a bridge sensor is easily drowned out by electromagnetic interference (like 50/60 Hz power line hum) and the bridge's own DC offset. Both of these appear equally on both sensor lines as common-mode voltage.

The three-op-amp architecture excels at eliminating this noise through a two-step process:

  1. The Input Stage: The two front-end op-amps are cross-coupled through a shared gain resistor. Because of how the feedback loops interact, the input stage amplifies the differential microvolt signal by a massive factor while passing the common-mode voltage through at a gain of just 1 (unity gain).
  2. The Output Stage: The third op-amp is configured as a standard unity-gain difference amplifier. It subtracts the two buffered signals, effectively canceling out the unamplified common-mode voltage while passing the newly amplified differential signal to the output.

Because the internal resistors of the third stage are laser-trimmed at the factory for near-perfect matching, the three-op-amp in-amp achieves a remarkably high Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR), often exceeding 100 dB to 120 dB. Crucially, increasing the gain of the amplifier increases the CMRR, making it uniquely suited for microvolt signals requiring high amplification.

Single-Resistor Gain Control

To elevate a microvolt signal to a level usable by an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)—typically a few volts—high gain is required.

In a standard differential amplifier, changing the gain requires changing at least two resistors simultaneously while maintaining their exact ratio. Any slight mismatch in resistor tolerance drastically degrades the CMRR, allowing common-mode noise to ruin the microvolt reading.

The three-op-amp topology decouples gain control from common-mode rejection. The gain of the entire instrumentation amplifier is set by a single external resistor (\(R_G\)) placed between the inverting inputs of the first two op-amps. Designers can easily set gains from 1 to 10,000 using one precision resistor without affecting the laser-trimmed resistor matching of the output stage, keeping the CMRR intact.

Low Offset Drift and Noise Symmetry

When measuring microvolts, the internal flaws of the amplifier itself become significant. If an op-amp has an internal input offset voltage that drifts with temperature, the measurement system cannot distinguish between that drift and a real change in the bridge sensor.

Because the input stage of a three-op-amp in-amp is completely symmetrical and built on the same silicon die, the two input amplifiers share nearly identical thermal and electrical characteristics. As ambient temperatures fluctuate, the offset voltages and bias currents of both input op-amps drift at the same rate and in the same direction. The third op-amp views these identical drifts as a common-mode signal and subtracts them out. This inherent symmetry ensures that the microvolt baseline remains stable over time and temperature variations.
