Why Active PFC Is Required for Power Supplies Over 75W

International regulations require active boost power factor correction (PFC) in AC-DC power supplies rated above 75 watts to protect the electrical grid from harmonic distortion and wasted energy. Standard AC-DC power supplies draw current in short, high-amplitude pulses rather than a smooth wave, which degrades grid efficiency and overloads utility infrastructure. This article explains the mechanics of power factor, the specific international standards governing the 75-watt threshold, the problems caused by uncorrected power supplies, and why the active boost topology is the industry solution for compliance.

Understanding Power Factor and Harmonic Distortion

In an ideal electrical system, the alternating current (AC) drawn by a device is a smooth sine wave that aligns perfectly in phase with the grid's voltage sine wave. The ratio of real power (used to do work) to apparent power (total power supplied by the grid) is called the power factor. A perfect power factor is 1.0.

Basic AC-DC power supplies use a bridge rectifier followed by a large bulk storage capacitor to convert AC to direct current (DC). The capacitor only draws current from the grid when the AC line voltage exceeds the voltage currently stored in the capacitor. This happens only at the very peaks of the AC voltage cycle.

Consequently, the power supply draws current in narrow, high-current spikes rather than a continuous sine wave. These spikes create severe non-linear distortion known as Total Harmonic Distortion (THD). High THD results in a poor power factor, typically between 0.5 and 0.6 for an uncorrected power supply.

The 75-Watt Regulatory Threshold (IEC 61000-3-2)

To prevent millions of electronic devices from polluting the AC mains with harmonic currents, the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) established the IEC 61000-3-2 standard (adopted in Europe as EN 61000-3-2). This regulation sets strict limits on the harmonic current emissions that equipment can inject back into the public power grid.

Under IEC 61000-3-2, personal computers, monitors, and television receivers are categorized as Class D equipment. The regulation specifies that Class D devices drawing less than 75 watts are exempt from harmonic current limits. However, once a device exceeds the 75-watt input threshold, strict limits apply.

Regulators chose 75 watts as the cutoff because devices below this threshold generally do not draw enough current individually or collectively to destabilize the grid, and adding PFC components to low-power devices is not cost-effective. Above 75 watts—a category that includes desktop PCs, gaming consoles, large TVs, and servers—the cumulative impact of harmonic currents becomes significant enough to require intervention.

The Danger to the Electrical Grid

When thousands of power supplies over 75 watts operate without power factor correction, their distorted current spikes combine on the local utility grid, leading to several critical issues:

  • Overheated Neutral Wires: In three-phase power distribution systems used in commercial buildings, balanced linear loads cancel each other out in the neutral wire. However, the third-order harmonics generated by uncorrected power supplies do not cancel out. Instead, they add up in the neutral line, causing it to overheat and creating a fire hazard.
  • Transformer Stress: Harmonic currents cause eddy current losses and core heating in utility distribution transformers, reducing their lifespan and forcing utilities to derate their equipment.
  • Wasted Generation Capacity: Because the apparent power is much higher than the real power being consumed, power plants and transmission lines must carry extra current that performs no useful work. Utilities must build thicker cables and larger transformers just to handle the peak current spikes.
  • Voltage Distortion: High current spikes cause voltage drops across the impedance of the power lines, flattening the peaks of the AC voltage waveform. This "flat-topping" can cause other sensitive electronic equipment on the same circuit to malfunction.

Why Active Boost PFC Is the Required Solution

To comply with the 75-watt mandate, engineers must reshape the input current into a sinusoidal wave. While passive PFC (using large inductors) is possible, the inductors required for power supplies over 75 watts are too heavy, bulky, and inefficient for modern electronics, especially when accommodating universal AC inputs (85V to 265V).

Active PFC solves this by placing a high-frequency switching converter between the bridge rectifier and the bulk capacitor. The boost converter topology is the industry standard for this stage for the following reasons:

  • Continuous Input Current: A boost converter features an inductor directly in series with the input line. Because current through an inductor cannot change instantaneously, the circuit can draw a continuous, smooth current from the AC mains rather than sharp spikes.
  • Precise Wave Shaping: An active PFC controller chip monitors the incoming rectified AC voltage and rapidly switches a MOSFET on and off (typically at tens or hundreds of kilohertz). By adjusting the duty cycle of this switch, the controller forces the average input current to follow the exact sinusoidal shape and phase of the input voltage.
  • High Power Factor: Active boost PFC easily achieves a power factor of 0.95 to 0.99, reducing Total Harmonic Distortion to well below the limits required by IEC 61000-3-2.
  • Universal Input Compatibility: A boost topology steps up the voltage to a regulated high DC level (usually around 380V to 400V DC), which is higher than the peak voltage of a 265V AC line. This allows the downstream DC-DC converter to be optimized for a single input voltage, making the power supply smaller, more efficient, and automatically compatible with any power grid worldwide.
