Why Does a Buck-Boost Converter Invert Output Voltage?

This article explains why a conventional buck-boost converter produces an output voltage with the opposite polarity of its input voltage. By examining the circuit's topology, the physical behavior of inductors, and the two distinct operating states of the switching cycle, you will understand how current flow direction forces this voltage inversion in power electronics.

The Core Component Layout

To understand the polarity reversal, you must first look at how the components are arranged relative to the circuit's ground. A conventional inverting buck-boost converter consists of four primary components:

  • A Switch (usually a MOSFET): Placed in series with the input voltage source.
  • An Inductor: Connected in parallel between the switch node and the ground reference.
  • A Diode: Oriented to block current from flowing into the output while the switch is closed, meaning its anode points toward the load and its cathode connects to the switch-inductor node.
  • An Output Capacitor: Placed in parallel with the load to filter the output voltage.

The orientation of the inductor and the diode is the ultimate reason the output voltage becomes negative relative to the common ground.

State 1: The On-State (Charging the Inductor)

When the switch closes (turns on), the circuit enters the charging phase.

  1. The positive input voltage source is connected directly across the inductor.
  2. Current flows from the positive input terminal, through the switch, and down through the inductor to the ground.
  3. As this downward current increases, the inductor stores energy in a magnetic field. At this moment, the top of the inductor is positive, and the bottom (ground side) is negative.
  4. Because the diode is reverse-biased by the positive input voltage, no current flows to the output load during this phase. The load is powered entirely by the energy previously stored in the output capacitor.

State 2: The Off-State (Discharging the Inductor)

When the switch opens (turns off), the input voltage source is completely disconnected from the rest of the circuit. This triggers the physical property of inductors that causes the voltage inversion.

  1. Inductor Current Continuity: A fundamental rule of inductors is that the current flowing through them cannot change instantaneously. Because current was flowing downward toward the ground during the On-State, it must continue flowing downward during the Off-State.
  2. Back-EMF Generation: To keep the current flowing in the same downward direction without the input source, the collapsing magnetic field causes the inductor to reverse its own voltage polarity (generating Back-EMF). The bottom of the inductor (connected to ground) becomes the positive terminal, and the top node becomes negative.
  3. Current Path Through the Load: Because the inductor's current is flowing down to the ground, it needs a closed loop to return to the top of the inductor. With the switch open, the only available path is through the output capacitor, the load, and the diode.
  4. Reverse Current Flow: The inductor forces current out of its bottom terminal (ground), up through the parallel load and output capacitor, and through the now forward-biased diode back to the top of the inductor.

Why the Polarity is Opposite

Voltage polarity across a passive load (like a resistor or capacitor) is determined by the direction the current enters it. By convention, the point where current enters a load is positive, and the point where it exits is negative.

In the Off-State of a conventional buck-boost converter, the inductor pulls current upward from the ground reference, through the load, and back to the top switch node.

Because the current enters the bottom of the load (which is tied to the 0V ground reference) and exits at the top (the output terminal), the ground node is effectively more positive than the output terminal. Since the ground is held at 0V, the output terminal is forced to a negative potential. Consequently, a positive input voltage always yields a negative output voltage.
