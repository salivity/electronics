Why Does SAC305 Solder Require Higher Reflow Temperatures?

This article explains why tin-silver-copper (SAC305) lead-free solder requires higher reflow peak temperatures than classic eutectic tin-lead (Sn63/Pb37) solder in electronics manufacturing. The primary reasons stem from the fundamental metallurgical properties of the alloys, specifically the higher melting point of pure tin compared to lead-mixed alloys, as well as the differences in wetting behavior and surface tension. By reading further, you will understand how alloy composition dictates thermal profiles and why the extra heat is necessary to form reliable intermetallic bonds in modern lead-free circuit boards.

The most direct reason SAC305 requires a higher reflow temperature is its higher melting point, known as the liquidus temperature. Classic eutectic tin-lead solder consists of 63% tin and 37% lead, a specific ratio that melts cleanly at a single temperature of 183°C. When lead was removed from electronics due to environmental regulations like RoHS, manufacturers turned to SAC305, which consists of 96.5% tin, 3.0% silver, and 0.5% copper. Because pure tin melts at 232°C, an alloy made almost entirely of tin naturally inherits a much higher melting threshold. Adding small amounts of silver and copper lowers the melting point slightly, resulting in a SAC305 melting range of 217°C to 220°C. This is an immediate baseline increase of nearly 37°C before reflow profiling even begins.

Melting the solder is only the first step; achieving a reliable mechanical and electrical bond requires going well above the melting point. In reflow soldering, the peak temperature must exceed the alloy's liquidus temperature to create a "superheat" zone. This excess heat is required to overcome the thermal mass of the printed circuit board and the electronic components, ensuring that the coldest spot on the board still reaches the melting point of the solder paste. For tin-lead solder, a peak temperature of 210°C to 225°C is sufficient. For SAC305, the peak temperature must be pushed to 235°C to 250°C to achieve the same thermal equilibrium across the assembly.

Wetting capability and surface tension also dictate the need for higher heat in lead-free processes. Lead is an exceptional metal for soldering because it reduces the surface tension of molten tin, allowing the liquid solder to flow smoothly and spread easily across copper pads and component leads. SAC305 lacks lead, making the molten solder much more viscous with a higher surface tension. It resists spreading naturally. Increasing the peak reflow temperature lowers the viscosity of the molten SAC305 and accelerates the chemical reaction of the flux, forcing the lead-free solder to wet the surfaces adequately.

Finally, the higher temperature is critical for forming the intermetallic compound (IMC) layer. A true solder joint is not just glue; it is a metallurgical bond formed when the tin in the solder reacts with the copper on the circuit board to create a thin copper-tin alloy layer. Because SAC305 has poorer wetting characteristics and a higher melting point, it requires elevated temperatures and precise time above liquidus (TAL) to drive the diffusion of tin and copper atoms. Without reaching a peak temperature of around 240°C to 250°C, the intermetallic layer will not form properly, resulting in "cold" solder joints that are brittle and prone to early failure.
