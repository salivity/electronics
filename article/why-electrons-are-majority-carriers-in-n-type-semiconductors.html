Why Electrons Are Majority Carriers in N-Type Semiconductors

This article provides a comprehensive overview of why electrons serve as the majority charge carriers in phosphorus-doped N-type semiconductors. We will explore the atomic structure of pure silicon, the process of doping it with pentavalent phosphorus, and how the resulting covalent bonds leave behind excess free electrons. By understanding the difference between the number of donated electrons and naturally occurring holes, it becomes clear how these materials achieve high electrical conductivity in modern electronics.

Pure silicon is the foundational material for most semiconductors. A silicon atom has four valence electrons in its outer shell. In a crystalline structure, each silicon atom forms a covalent bond with four neighboring silicon atoms, sharing these electrons to create a stable eight-electron configuration. In this intrinsic state, there are very few free electrons available to conduct electricity, making pure silicon a poor conductor.

To increase conductivity, manufacturers introduce impurities into the silicon crystal through a process called doping. To create an N-type (negative-type) semiconductor, silicon is doped with phosphorus. Phosphorus is a pentavalent element, meaning it has five valence electrons in its outer shell instead of four.

When a phosphorus atom is introduced into the silicon crystal lattice, it takes the place of a silicon atom. Four of the phosphorus atom's पांच valence electrons form stable covalent bonds with the four surrounding silicon atoms. However, the fifth valence electron has no silicon electron to bond with.

Because the covalent bonds around the phosphorus atom are already complete, this fifth electron is only very weakly bound to the parent phosphorus atom. At room temperature, the small amount of available thermal energy is enough to break this weak attraction, freeing the fifth electron to move into the conduction band. Because phosphorus donates a free, mobile electron to the crystal structure without creating a corresponding positive hole, it is referred to as a donor impurity.

In any semiconductor at room temperature, a small amount of thermal energy will also break a few native silicon-to-silicon bonds, creating pairs of free electrons and positively charged "holes" (spaces where an electron is missing). However, in a phosphorus-doped N-type semiconductor, the millions of free electrons intentionally added by the phosphorus atoms vastly outnumber the thermally generated holes.

Furthermore, the massive influx of free electrons from the phosphorus increases the likelihood that these electrons will fall into and eliminate the few existing holes, a process known as recombination. This actively reduces the hole population even further.

Ultimately, electrical current in this material is driven almost entirely by the movement of negative charges. Because the concentration of negatively charged free electrons is exponentially higher than the concentration of positively charged holes, electrons are designated as the majority charge carriers, while the scarce holes are known as the minority charge carriers.
