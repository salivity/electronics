Why Half-Wave Dipole Antennas Have a 73 Ohm Resistance

A resonant half-wave dipole antenna in free space presents a radiation resistance of approximately 73 ohms due to the fundamental physics of electromagnetic wave propagation and the geometry of the antenna itself. This article provides a quick overview of how current and voltage distributions along a half-wavelength wire interact with the intrinsic impedance of free space. By calculating the total power radiated into the surrounding environment and relating it to the input current at the center feed point, engineers and physicists consistently arrive at the standard 73.13-ohm value.

To understand where the 73-ohm figure comes from, you first need to understand radiation resistance. Unlike standard electrical resistance, which converts electrical energy into heat, radiation resistance is a virtual resistance. It represents the transfer of energy from the antenna into free space as electromagnetic radio waves. It is defined as the value of a hypothetical resistor that would dissipate the same amount of power as the antenna radiates when driven by the same current.

When an alternating current is fed into the center of a half-wave dipole, it creates standing waves of current and voltage along the two quarter-wave elements. Because the ends of the wire are open circuits, the current must drop to zero at the tips. Consequently, the current reaches its maximum value right at the center feed point. Conversely, the voltage is at its maximum at the tips and drops to a minimum (though not entirely zero) at the center. Because Impedance (\(Z\)) equals Voltage (\(V\)) divided by Current (\(I\)), the center of the dipole exhibits a relatively low impedance.

The next critical factor is the impedance of free space, which is approximately 377 ohms. This value is a universal physical constant derived from the permeability and permittivity of a vacuum. It dictates the ratio of the electric field to the magnetic field in an electromagnetic wave traveling through empty space. The dipole antenna acts as a transformer, coupling the electrical energy on the feedline to this 377-ohm environment.

To find the exact radiation resistance, physicists calculate the total power the antenna radiates in all directions. A half-wave dipole does not radiate equally in a sphere; instead, it radiates in a doughnut-shaped pattern (toroid) perpendicular to the wire. By using Poynting's theorem, you can integrate the radiated power density over a giant imaginary sphere surrounding the antenna in the far-field.

Because the current along the dipole follows a sinusoidal distribution, the mathematical integration of this power over the entire sphere involves complex calculus, specifically utilizing cosine integrals. When you solve the integral for the total radiated power (\(P\)) of an infinitely thin half-wave dipole in free space, the equation simplifies to:

\(P = I_{rms}^2 \times 73.13\)

In basic electronics, power is calculated as the root-mean-square current squared, multiplied by resistance (\(P = I_{rms}^2 \times R\)). By comparing the two equations, it becomes clear that the radiation resistance (\(R\)) at the center feed point is 73.13 ohms.

In practical radio electronics, this 73-ohm value is only entirely accurate for an infinitely thin wire suspended in a complete vacuum, infinitely far from the ground. Furthermore, an exact physical half-wave wire actually has a slight inductive reactance of about +42.5 ohms, making the total impedance \(73 + j42.5\) ohms. To make the antenna purely resonant (eliminating the reactance), engineers shorten the physical wire by about 5 percent. This slight shortening reduces the radiation resistance to roughly 68 to 70 ohms, which is why 75-ohm and 50-ohm coaxial cables are the standard feedlines used in radio communication today.
