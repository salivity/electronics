Why Kirchhoff's Voltage Law Equals Zero in Closed Loops

This article explains the fundamental physics behind Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL), illustrating why the algebraic sum of potential differences in any closed electrical loop must always equal zero. It explores the direct relationship between KVL and the Law of Conservation of Energy, details how electric potential functions as a conservative field, and provides a clear analogy to show how voltage drops and rises balance out within an electronic circuit.

The Law of Conservation of Energy

The primary reason the algebraic sum of potential differences around any closed loop equals zero is the Law of Conservation of Energy. This fundamental principle of physics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only change from one form to another.

In electronics, electric potential difference—commonly known as voltage—is a measure of potential energy per unit of charge. As electric charges move through a closed circuit, they gain energy from power sources (like batteries or generators) and lose energy as they pass through loads (like resistors, LEDs, or motors) which convert electrical energy into heat, light, or motion. Because energy cannot mysteriously appear or vanish, the total energy gained by a charge in one complete trip around a loop must exactly equal the total energy lost.

Electric Potential as a Conservative Field

In steady-state circuits, the electric field is conservative. This means that the work done on a charge moving between two points depends only on the starting and ending locations, not on the path taken between them.

Every specific point in an electrical circuit has a single, unique electric potential value at any given instant. If you take a test charge from a specific starting point, move it through various components around a closed loop, and return it to the exact same starting point, its final potential energy must be identical to its initial potential energy. Because the starting point and the ending point are the exact same location, the net change in electric potential between the start and the end of the journey is zero.

Balancing Voltage Rises and Voltage Drops

To apply this mathematically, Kirchhoff's Voltage Law uses an algebraic sum, which accounts for both positive and negative values:

  • Voltage Rise (+): When a charge passes through a power source from the negative terminal to the positive terminal, its potential energy increases. This is recorded as a positive value.
  • Voltage Drop (-): When a charge passes through a passive component like a resistor, it expends energy to overcome electrical resistance. Its potential energy decreases, which is recorded as a negative value.

If a 9-volt battery is connected to a closed loop containing two identical resistors, the charge gains 9 volts of potential at the battery. As it travels through the first resistor, it drops 4.5 volts. As it travels through the second resistor, it drops the remaining 4.5 volts. The algebraic sum is +9V - 4.5V - 4.5V = 0V.

The Elevation Analogy

The clearest way to understand this concept is by comparing electrical potential to gravitational potential, such as hiking on a mountain.

Imagine you start a hike at a base camp located at 1,000 feet above sea level. During your hike, you climb up steep hills (voltage rises) and walk down into valleys (voltage drops). Regardless of how many miles you walk or how many peaks you scale, if you return to the exact same base camp at the end of the day, your final elevation is still 1,000 feet. The algebraic sum of all your elevation gains and elevation losses throughout the closed-loop hike must equal zero.

If the sum of the voltages in a closed electrical loop did not equal zero, it would be the equivalent of returning to the exact same base camp and somehow standing at a different altitude. In electronics, a non-zero sum would mean charges are spontaneously gaining or losing energy on every lap around the circuit, violating the laws of physics.
