Why Metal Oxide Varistors Are Used Across Power Inputs

This article explains why engineers place metal oxide varistors (MOVs) across power inputs in electronic devices. It covers the fundamental behavior of MOVs as voltage-dependent resistors, how they rapidly react to clamp dangerous voltage spikes, and their critical role in working alongside fuses to protect sensitive downstream circuitry from electrical surges and transient events.

An electronic device connected to an external power source is constantly vulnerable to transient voltage spikes. These spikes can be caused by lightning strikes, electrostatic discharge, or inductive loads switching on and off on the same power grid. Without protection, these sudden surges of high voltage will enter the power input and destroy delicate internal components like microcontrollers and power supply regulators. Metal oxide varistors are placed directly across the power input lines to act as the first line of defense against these destructive transients.

A metal oxide varistor is a voltage-dependent, nonlinear resistor composed primarily of zinc oxide grains pressed into a ceramic mass. Its defining characteristic is that its electrical resistance changes dramatically based on the voltage applied to it. Under normal operating voltages, the MOV exhibits extremely high resistance, functioning almost like an open circuit. Because it is placed in parallel across the power input—typically from the live wire to the neutral wire, or from the power lines to the ground—it draws virtually no current during standard operation and remains invisible to the rest of the circuit.

When a voltage spike enters the power input and exceeds the MOV's specific "clamping voltage" threshold, the properties of the component change within nanoseconds. The resistance of the MOV drops from millions of ohms to just a few ohms. By becoming a path of very low resistance, the MOV creates a temporary short circuit across the power input lines.

This sudden drop in resistance diverts the massive surge current away from the sensitive downstream electronics and channels it safely back to the neutral or ground line. In doing so, the MOV "clamps" the voltage, restricting the maximum voltage that can pass into the rest of the circuit to a safe, predetermined level. The excess electrical energy from the spike is absorbed by the MOV and dissipated harmlessly as a brief pulse of heat. Once the voltage spike subsides and the input power returns to normal levels, the MOV instantly reverts to its high-resistance state, allowing the device to continue functioning without interruption.

In addition to clamping brief transients, MOVs are placed at the power input to work in tandem with input fuses during sustained overvoltage events. An MOV can only absorb a finite amount of energy before it overheats and degrades. If a massive or prolonged power surge occurs, the MOV will conduct so much current that it forces the primary fuse placed just before it to blow. By sacrificing itself to trip the fuse, the MOV physically disconnects the circuit from the hazardous power source, preventing catastrophic damage or fire within the electronic device.
