Why Place a GaAs LNA Directly After the Antenna?

This article explains why a Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) must be placed immediately after the antenna to establish the system noise floor in receiver electronics. When a radio frequency signal is captured by an antenna, it is extremely weak and susceptible to degradation. Placing a GaAs LNA as the very first active component maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio by boosting the desired signal while introducing minimal internal noise. This strategic placement utilizes Friis's formula for noise, ensuring that the higher noise figures of downstream components—such as mixers, filters, and analog-to-digital converters—do not corrupt the system's overall sensitivity.

The Physics of the System Noise Floor

In any radio frequency (RF) receiver, the system noise floor determines the minimum signal strength the receiver can successfully detect and process. Signals arriving at the antenna have often traveled vast distances, making them exceptionally weak and hovering just above the natural thermal noise floor.

Once the signal enters the receiver hardware, every component it passes through—including cables, waveguides, filters, and mixers—does two things: it alters the signal strength, and it adds its own internal electronic noise. If the weak signal is not amplified immediately, the internal noise of the receiver will easily overwhelm it, rendering the data unrecoverable.

Friis's Formula and the First-Stage Dominance

To understand why the LNA must go first, engineers rely on Friis's formula for noise factor. The formula calculates the total noise factor (\(F_{total}\)) of a cascaded system of components:

\[F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1 G_2} + \dots\]

In this equation, \(F\) represents the noise factor of each stage, and \(G\) represents the linear gain of each stage.

The equation proves that the total noise of the entire receiver is dominated by the noise factor of the very first component (\(F_1\)). Because the noise of the second stage (\(F_2\)) is divided by the gain of the first stage (\(G_1\)), having a high-gain, low-noise component at the very beginning effectively shrinks the noise contributions of every subsequent component to near zero. Therefore, the LNA sets the noise floor for the entire system.

The Danger of Pre-LNA Attenuation

If passive components like long coaxial cables or complex filter banks are placed between the antenna and the LNA, the system noise floor degrades rapidly. In RF engineering, any passive loss (attenuation) before the first amplifier adds directly, decibel for decibel, to the system's overall noise figure.

Furthermore, passive loss reduces the gain (\(G_1\)) to a fraction less than one. According to Friis's formula, dividing the second stage's noise by a fraction actually multiplies the noise of the downstream components. By placing the LNA directly at the antenna terminals, you eliminate pre-amplifier transmission loss and lock in the lowest possible noise floor before the signal has to travel down a cable to the rest of the receiver.

Why Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) is Essential

While placement is dictated by system architecture, the material of the LNA dictates its baseline performance (\(F_1\) and \(G_1\)). Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) is overwhelmingly chosen over standard silicon for the first-stage LNA for several physical reasons:

  • Superior Electron Mobility: Electrons move through a GaAs crystal lattice up to six times faster than they do through silicon. This allows GaAs transistors to switch much faster, making them ideal for high-frequency, microwave, and millimeter-wave applications.
  • Lower Parasitic Capacitance: GaAs substrates are semi-insulating, whereas silicon substrates are semi-conducting. This insulating property drastically reduces parasitic capacitance, preventing high-frequency signals from leaking into the substrate and being lost.
  • Reduced Thermal and Shot Noise: Because electrons move through GaAs with less resistance and scattering, the material generates significantly less internal thermal noise.

By combining the physical properties of Gallium Arsenide with immediate post-antenna placement, the LNA provides a massive gain (\(G_1\)) alongside a microscopic noise factor (\(F_1\)). This combination locks in an ultra-low system noise floor, allowing the receiver to clearly resolve the faintest possible incoming signals.
