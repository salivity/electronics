Why Precision LCR Bridges Use Multiple Test Frequencies

This article explains why precision LCR bridges use multiple alternating current (AC) test frequencies to measure electronic components rather than relying on simple direct current (DC) resistance. You will learn how capacitors and inductors react dynamically to changing frequencies, the critical role of parasitic elements, and why testing components at their actual operating frequencies is essential for accurate circuit design and quality control.

The Limitations of DC Resistance

Simple DC resistance measurements, such as those taken by a standard digital multimeter, only measure how a material opposes a steady, unchanging flow of current. While this is sufficient for basic resistors, it fails to characterize passive components that store energy, such as inductors and capacitors.

Under DC conditions, an ideal capacitor acts as an open circuit (infinite resistance) once charged, and an ideal inductor acts as a short circuit (zero resistance) aside from the minor resistance of its wire windings. To measure the actual capacitance or inductance, an AC signal must be applied so the LCR bridge can measure impedance—the total opposition to current flow, which includes both resistance and reactance.

Frequency-Dependent Reactance

Reactance is the opposition to AC current caused by inductance and capacitance, and it changes drastically depending on the frequency of the test signal.

  • Capacitive Reactance (\(X_C\)): Decreases as frequency increases. A capacitor blocks DC, passes low-frequency signals poorly, and passes high-frequency signals easily.
  • Inductive Reactance (\(X_L\)): Increases as frequency increases. An inductor passes DC easily, impedes low-frequency signals slightly, and strongly blocks high-frequency signals.

Because reactance is tied directly to frequency, measuring a component at a single frequency only gives a snapshot of its behavior. Precision LCR bridges sweep across multiple frequencies to map out a component's exact reactance profile.

Uncovering Parasitic Elements

No electronic component is ideal. Every real-world component contains unwanted "parasitic" elements created by its physical construction and materials:

  • Resistors have parasitic inductance from their leads and parasitic capacitance between their windings or terminals.
  • Capacitors have Equivalent Series Resistance (ESR) and Equivalent Series Inductance (ESL).
  • Inductors have winding resistance and parasitic capacitance between the coils.

At DC or very low frequencies, these parasitics are practically invisible. However, as frequencies increase, parasitic elements begin to dominate the component's behavior. Every capacitor and inductor has a Self-Resonant Frequency (SRF). Above its SRF, a capacitor actually begins to behave like an inductor due to its ESL, and an inductor begins to behave like a capacitor due to its inter-winding capacitance. Multi-frequency LCR testing identifies the exact frequency where a component ceases to function as intended.

Material Core Losses and Skin Effect

Testing across multiple frequencies also reveals how component materials react to AC signals.

In inductors, the magnetic core material experiences permeability changes and core losses (eddy currents and hysteresis) that increase with frequency. In capacitors, the dielectric material experiences varying dissipation factors (energy lost as heat) depending on the applied frequency. Furthermore, at high frequencies, the skin effect forces AC current to flow only on the outer surface of a conductor, effectively increasing the resistance of component leads and windings in a way that DC testing can never detect.

Matching Real-World Operating Conditions

Electronic circuits operate at vastly different frequencies. A power supply filter capacitor operates at 50 Hz or 60 Hz, a switching regulator inductor might operate at 500 kHz, and radio frequency (RF) components operate in the megahertz or gigahertz ranges.

A capacitor that measures 10 microfarads at 100 Hz might only yield an effective capacitance of 2 microfarads at 100 kHz. Precision LCR bridges use multiple test frequencies so engineers can measure impedance, phase angle, ESR, and quality factor (\(Q\)) at the exact frequency the component will experience in its final circuit application, ensuring reliable real-world performance.
