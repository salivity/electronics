Why Through-Hole Mounting Is Used in Rugged Electronics

Plated through-hole (PTH) mounting remains essential in rugged electronics due to its superior mechanical strength compared to surface mount technology (SMT). This article explores why engineers continue to rely on PTH for heavy transformers and high-stress external connectors. Key factors include the prevention of copper pad delamination, resistance to intense shock and vibration, and protection against physical stress from repeated cable insertion and removal.

Superior Mechanical Anchoring

Surface mount components are soldered solely to the thin copper pads on the outer layer of a printed circuit board (PCB). The structural integrity of an SMT joint is limited by the adhesive strength binding that copper foil to the underlying fiberglass substrate.

In contrast, plated through-hole mounting requires component leads to pass completely through drilled holes in the PCB. When molten solder fills the plated barrel, it encapsulates the lead from the top layer to the bottom layer. This forms a continuous, solid metal column that anchors the component directly into the structural core of the board, rather than resting on its surface.

Supporting Heavy Transformers

Power transformers and large inductors contain dense magnetic cores and heavy copper windings, giving them significant mass. In rugged applications—such as aerospace, military, and heavy industrial equipment—devices are constantly subjected to extreme gravitational forces, drops, and relentless vibration.

If a heavy transformer is mounted solely via surface pads, its inertia during a high-vibration event creates substantial shear and peel stresses. Over time, this movement causes the solder joints to fracture or rips the copper pads completely off the PCB substrate. PTH mounting mitigates this failure mode. Because the transformer’s leads pass through the board, the mechanical load is distributed across the entire thickness of the fiberglass substrate, locking the heavy component in place regardless of orientation or G-force.

Securing High-Stress External Connectors

External I/O connectors face direct, unpredictable mechanical abuse from human operators and heavy-duty cabling. Every time a user plugs in, unplugs, or accidentally trips over an attached cable, significant torque, bending, and pulling forces are transferred directly to the connector's mounting points on the PCB.

Surface mount solder joints have very low resistance to peeling forces. A sharp tug on a cable attached to an SMT connector can easily tear the connector away from the board, destroying the electrical traces in the process. Through-hole mounting acts as a physical cleat. To rip a PTH connector off a board, the force must be great enough to either shear the solid metal pins in half or shatter the fiberglass board itself. Many rugged designs use a hybrid approach: SMT pins for the delicate data signals and heavy-duty PTH mounting tabs for the connector housing to absorb the physical strain.

Endurance in Harsh Thermal Environments

Rugged electronics frequently operate in environments with extreme temperature fluctuations. As temperatures cycle from freezing to scorching, the PCB substrate and the electronic components expand and contract at different rates. This phenomenon, known as a Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE) mismatch, puts immense strain on rigid solder joints.

PTH connections handle thermal expansion significantly better than SMT connections on large components. The longer leads of through-hole components offer a slight degree of mechanical compliance, flexing microscopically to absorb thermal stress. Furthermore, the massive volume of solder inside the plated hole resists thermal fatigue cracking far longer than the thin fillet of an SMT pad, ensuring long-term reliability in extreme climates.
