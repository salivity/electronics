Why Wi-Fi Uses OFDM to Combat Multipath Fading

Modern Wi-Fi transceivers rely on Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) to deliver high-speed, reliable wireless connectivity in complex environments. This article provides a comprehensive overview of how multipath fading degrades wireless signals, why traditional single-carrier modulation fails at high data rates, and how OFDM solves these issues by splitting data across multiple orthogonal subcarriers and utilizing a cyclic prefix to eliminate signal interference.

Understanding Multipath Fading

In any typical indoor or urban environment, wireless signals do not travel exclusively in a straight line from the transmitter to the receiver. Instead, the radio frequency (RF) waves bounce off walls, furniture, floors, and moving objects. As a result, multiple copies of the same transmitted signal arrive at the Wi-Fi receiver's antenna at slightly different times.

This phenomenon creates two major problems for wireless electronics:

  • Destructive Interference: Signals arriving out of phase can cancel each other out, causing severe drops in signal strength known as deep fades.
  • Inter-Symbol Interference (ISI): Because reflected signals travel longer paths, they arrive delayed. At high data rates, where data symbols are extremely short, a delayed copy of one symbol will overlap and collide with the next incoming symbol, corrupting the data.

The Limitation of Single-Carrier Modulation

To transmit high-speed data using a single carrier frequency, the duration of each data symbol must be exceptionally brief. When the symbol duration is shorter than the delay spread (the time difference between the arrival of the first signal and the last reflected signal), Inter-Symbol Interference becomes unavoidable. Correcting this distortion on a single wide channel requires extremely complex and power-hungry equalization circuitry in the transceiver.

How OFDM Solves the Multipath Problem

Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) resolves the issue of multipath fading by fundamentally changing how data is transmitted across a wireless channel. Instead of sending a single, fast stream of data over one wide frequency band, OFDM divides the available bandwidth into dozens or hundreds of narrow, closely spaced sub-channels called subcarriers.

1. Slower Symbol Rates Reduce Interference By transmitting data in parallel across many subcarriers simultaneously, the data rate on each individual subcarrier is drastically reduced. A slower data rate means the duration of each symbol is much longer. Because the symbol duration is now significantly longer than the multipath delay spread, the reflected signals only affect a tiny fraction of the overall symbol, drastically reducing Inter-Symbol Interference.

2. The Cyclic Prefix Eliminates ISI To completely eliminate the remaining overlap between symbols, OFDM employs a mechanism called a cyclic prefix ( or guard interval). Before transmitting an OFDM symbol, the transceiver takes a copy of the end of the symbol and attaches it to the beginning.

This guard interval acts as a buffer zone. When multipath reflections from the previous symbol arrive late at the receiver, they land safely within this cyclic prefix rather than the active data period. The Wi-Fi receiver simply discards the cyclic prefix, removing the corrupted overlap and leaving the pure, uncorrupted data symbol intact.

3. Converting Frequency-Selective Fading to Flat Fading In a wide wireless channel, multipath fading affects different frequencies unevenly—a problem known as frequency-selective fading. Some parts of the channel may experience severe signal cancellation while others remain untouched.

Because OFDM slices the wide channel into many narrow subcarriers, the bandwidth of each subcarrier is small enough that it experiences "flat fading." This means the signal degradation is uniform across each individual subcarrier. For a Wi-Fi transceiver, correcting flat fading requires simple, single-tap equalizers in the digital signal processor, keeping the hardware fast, compact, and energy-efficient.

4. High Spectral Efficiency via Orthogonality Normally, placing multiple subcarriers close together would result in crosstalk, or Inter-Carrier Interference (ICI). OFDM prevents this through mathematical orthogonality. The frequencies of the subcarriers are chosen so that the peak of each subcarrier signal aligns precisely with the zero-crossings (nulls) of all adjacent subcarriers.

Using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms, the Wi-Fi transceiver can pack these subcarriers tightly together without them interfering with one another. If multipath fading completely destroys a specific frequency, only the data on that single subcarrier is lost, which can easily be recovered using Forward Error Correction (FEC) coding without dropping the entire Wi-Fi connection.
